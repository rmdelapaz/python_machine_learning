🧭 Know Normal, Flag the Rest

Pinecrest's online shop takes thousands of payments a month, and a small share are fraudulent: stolen cards used to buy gift cards and resellable gear. The payments team can review only a handful of orders a day. Which ones?

💡 Mental Model: The Night Guard

A night guard in a big building does not carry a list of every possible burglary. Instead, after a few weeks on the job, they know what normal sounds like: the hum of the server room, the cleaner at 2 a.m., the heating that clicks on at five. Anything that does not fit gets a closer look. Most of those closer looks turn out to be nothing, and that is fine; the point is to look in the right places.

Anomaly detection works the same way. A model learns the shape of normal data, then gives every new case an anomaly score: how far it is from normal. You decide how many of the highest scores you can afford to check.

Anomalies come in three flavors. A point anomaly is unusual on its own: a $3,000 order when almost all are under $500. A contextual anomaly is only unusual in context: $400 is ordinary for a tent but strange for a pair of socks, and a purchase at 3 a.m. is odder from an account that always shops at lunchtime. A collective anomaly is a group of cases that are each ordinary but unusual together, such as seven small orders from one account in a day. Good features turn the second and third kinds into the first: the data you will use includes velocity_24h, a count of the account's recent orders, for exactly this reason.

🤔 Why This Matters

Labels for rare events are scarce, slow and biased: a fraud is only labeled after a chargeback weeks later, and only the kinds of fraud someone noticed get labeled at all. An unsupervised detector needs no labels, so it works on day one and can flag a new kind of fraud that no classifier has seen. Where labels do exist, the honest question is how much they add, and you will measure it in the project.

📏 Simple Statistical Rules

Start with the simplest detector there is: one column and a threshold. Load the payments and look at how rare fraud is.

import numpy as np

pd.set_option("display.width", 150)

Under 1% of payments are fraud. Amounts are skewed: the mean is above the median and the maximum is far out. Two classic rules flag "extreme" amounts:

  • The z-score rule: flag values more than 3 standard deviations from the mean.
  • The IQR rule (Tukey's fences): flag values more than 1.5 interquartile ranges above the third quartile (or below the first). It uses quartiles, so the extreme values themselves cannot drag the threshold around the way they drag a mean and a standard deviation.
amount = tx["amount"]
z = (amount - amount.mean()) / amount.std()
q1, q3 = amount.quantile([0.25, 0.75])
upper_fence = q3 + 1.5 * (q3 - q1)

rules = {"z-score above 3": z > 3, f"IQR: amount above ${upper_fence:.0f}": amount > upper_fence}
for name, flagged in rules.items():
    print(f"{name:26s} flags {flagged.sum():5d}, fraud among flagged {tx.loc[flagged, 'is_fraud'].mean():.1%}, "
          f"frauds caught {tx.loc[flagged, 'is_fraud'].sum()} of {tx['is_fraud'].sum()}")

Output

z-score above 3            flags   909, fraud among flagged 4.7%, frauds caught 43 of 438
IQR: amount above $482     flags  2693, fraud among flagged 4.3%, frauds caught 115 of 438

Both rules raise the fraud rate among flagged payments well above the 0.9% base rate, so large amounts are a real signal. But they miss most frauds, because plenty of fraud is not large, and they flag hundreds or thousands of honest big purchases (tents and packs are expensive). A single column cannot see combinations such as "a mid-sized gift card bought at 3 a.m. on a brand-new device". For that you need detectors that look at all the features at once.

⚠️ Rules Are Still Worth Having

Simple rules are transparent, cheap and easy to explain to a customer whose order was held. Real fraud systems keep a few alongside models. Treat them as the baseline every model must beat, just as DummyClassifier was for classifiers.

🌲 Isolation Forest

Isolation Forest turns the question around. Instead of modeling what normal looks like, it asks how easy each point is to isolate. Pick a random feature and a random cut point between its minimum and maximum; keep the side that holds your point; repeat until the point is alone. A point in a crowd needs many cuts. An unusual point is cut off in very few.

You can see the idea with nothing but NumPy: 30 ordinary order amounts and one huge one.

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
amounts = np.append(rng.normal(150, 40, size=30).round(2), 900.0)


def isolation_depth(values, target, rng):
    """Random cuts needed until target is the only value left."""
    depth = 0
    while len(values) > 1:
        cut = rng.uniform(values.min(), values.max())
        values = values[values < cut] if target < cut else values[values >= cut]
        depth += 1
    return depth


for target in [np.median(amounts), 900.0]:
    depths = [isolation_depth(amounts, target, rng) for _ in range(1000)]
    print(f"amount {target:7.2f}: average cuts to isolate {np.mean(depths):.2f}")

Output

amount  155.11: average cuts to isolate 7.55
amount  900.00: average cuts to isolate 1.15

The typical amount needs about seven random cuts; the $900 amount is usually isolated by the very first one. An Isolation Forest does this with many random trees on random subsamples, in all features at once, and averages the depth: short average paths mean anomalies. It needs no distances, so it is fast, works with features on different scales, and handles tens of thousands of rows in a couple of seconds.

Now on two dimensions. The generated data below has a tight cluster, a loose cluster, and five planted oddballs. One of them sits just outside the tight cluster: close to normal points in absolute terms, but far from its own neighborhood.

from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest
from sklearn.neighbors import LocalOutlierFactor

X_toy, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=[300, 200], centers=[[0, 0], [6, 6]], cluster_std=[0.4, 1.6], random_state=42)

print(pd.concat([pd.DataFrame(planted, columns=["x", "y"]), ranks.tail(5).reset_index(drop=True)], axis=1))

🏘️ Local Outlier Factor

Local Outlier Factor (LOF) compares the density around each point with the density around its n_neighbors nearest neighbors. A point in a sparse cluster is fine if its neighbors are just as spread out. A point whose neighbors are packed much more tightly than it is (like the point next to the tight cluster) gets a high factor. Values near 1 mean "as dense as my neighbors"; values well above 1 mean "much sparser than my neighbors".

graph TD A["Find each point's k nearest neighbors"] --> B["Estimate local density of each point"] B --> C["Compare with the neighbors' densities"] C --> D{"Much sparser than its neighbors?"} D -->|"yes"| E["High LOF: outlier"] D -->|"no, about the same"| F["LOF near 1: normal"]

That local view is LOF's strength and its cost. It needs nearest neighbors, so it is slower on large data, and it depends on distances, so features must be scaled. It also has two modes that trip people up:

  • LocalOutlierFactor() (the default, novelty=False) scores the training data itself, through fit_predict and the attribute negative_outlier_factor_. It has no predict for new data.
  • LocalOutlierFactor(novelty=True) learns from training data and scores new rows with score_samples, decision_function and predict. Use this mode inside a pipeline that is fitted on one month and applied to the next.

💡 A Third Detector You Already Know

The Gaussian mixture from Lesson 8.5 is also a detector: score_samples gives each point's log-density, and the lowest densities are the anomalies. scikit-learn also has OneClassSVM (a boundary around the normal data; slow on large data) and EllipticEnvelope (one Gaussian, so only for a single roughly bell-shaped cloud). The project compares Isolation Forest, LOF and a mixture on the same payments.

🎚️ Scores, Thresholds and contamination

scikit-learn's outlier detectors share one interface, with a sign convention you have to learn once:

MethodReturnsConvention
score_samples(X)raw normality scorehigher = more normal; negate it to get an anomaly score
decision_function(X)score_samples minus offset_negative = outlier, positive = inlier
predict(X)labels-1 = outlier, 1 = inlier (not 0 and 1)

The contamination setting is the expected share of outliers in the training data. It does not change the scores at all; it only sets offset_, the threshold that predict uses. With the default "auto", Isolation Forest uses a fixed offset from the original paper.

for contamination in ["auto", 0.01, 0.05]:
    model = IsolationForest(n_estimators=200, contamination=contamination, random_state=42).fit(X_toy)
    flagged = (model.predict(X_toy) == -1).sum()
    same_scores = np.allclose(model.score_samples(X_toy), iso_toy.score_samples(X_toy))
    print(f"contamination={str(contamination):5s} offset {model.offset_:.3f}  flagged {flagged:3d}  same scores: {same_scores}")

Output

contamination=auto  offset -0.500  flagged 103  same scores: True
contamination=0.01  offset -0.645  flagged   6  same scores: True
contamination=0.05  offset -0.591  flagged  26  same scores: True

Same forest, same scores, three different thresholds. So do not tune contamination to make a detector "better": it cannot change which points are ranked as most anomalous. Choose the threshold from what you can act on, typically "the top N scores per day that the team can review", which is what the project does.

📊 Judging a Detector

An unsupervised detector does not need labels to train, but you need some to judge it. Even a few hundred confirmed cases from past investigations are enough. Accuracy is useless here (a detector that flags nothing is 99.1% accurate on these payments). Use measures of how well the score ranks the anomalies, from Lessons 1.4 and 2.5:

  • ROC AUC: the chance that a random fraud scores higher than a random honest payment (0.5 = random ranking).
  • Average precision (AP, the area under the precision-recall curve): rewards putting frauds at the very top. For a random score it equals the fraud rate, about 0.009, so it is the stricter measure for rare events.
  • Catches within a review budget: if the team reviews the top 165 payments in a month, how many frauds are among them? This is the number the payments manager cares about.
graph TD A["Fit detector on past months, no labels"] --> B["Score the new month"] B --> C["Sort by anomaly score"] C --> D["Review the top N the team can handle"] D --> E["Confirmed frauds become labels"] E --> F["Measure catches; later, train a classifier"]

The loop at the bottom matters: every review produces labels, and once enough have accumulated, a supervised model can learn from them. The project shows how much that is worth on Pinecrest's data.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

1. Reading predict as 0 and 1

toy_pred = iso_toy.predict(X_toy)
print("labels returned:", np.unique(toy_pred))
print("wrong 'outliers found':", (toy_pred == 1).sum())
print("real outliers flagged :", (toy_pred == -1).sum())

Output (no error, and that is the problem)

labels returned: [-1  1]
wrong 'outliers found': 402
real outliers flagged : 103

What happened: outlier detectors return -1 for outliers and 1 for inliers. Code written for classifiers, such as (pred == 1).sum() or comparing with a 0/1 label column, silently counts the normal points instead. Fix: convert explicitly, is_outlier = pred == -1, and better still work with scores and choose your own threshold.

2. Using score_samples the Wrong Way Round

from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

print("AUC of score_samples         :", round(roc_auc_score(y_toy, iso_toy.score_samples(X_toy)), 3))
print("AUC of -score_samples        :", round(roc_auc_score(y_toy, -iso_toy.score_samples(X_toy)), 3))

Output (no error, and that is the problem)

AUC of score_samples         : 0.001
AUC of -score_samples        : 0.999

What happened: score_samples is a normality score, so the planted outliers get the lowest values. Fed straight into a metric that expects "higher = more anomalous", a good detector looks far worse than random. Fix: negate it (-model.score_samples(X)) once, name the result anomaly_score, and use that name everywhere. An AUC well below 0.5 is almost always a flipped sign.

3. Asking a Default LOF to Score New Data

lof_toy.predict(X_toy[:3])

Error

AttributeError: This 'LocalOutlierFactor' has no attribute 'predict'

What it means: with the default novelty=False, LOF only describes the data it was fitted on, so it offers fit_predict but no predict, score_samples or decision_function. Fix: create it with LocalOutlierFactor(novelty=True) when you fit on one period and score another, and never call predict on the training rows in that mode (their scores would be optimistic, because each point is its own neighbor).

4. Judging the Detector by Accuracy

all_normal_accuracy = 1 - tx["is_fraud"].mean()
print(f"accuracy of flagging nothing at all: {all_normal_accuracy:.4f}")

Output (no error, and that is the problem)

accuracy of flagging nothing at all: 0.9912

What happened: with fraud below 1%, doing nothing scores over 99% accuracy, so accuracy cannot tell a useful detector from a useless one. Fix: report ROC AUC, average precision and catches within the review budget, and always next to a baseline (the amount rule in the project).

5. Calling Every Anomaly a Fraud

An anomaly score measures unusual, not bad. A first-time customer buying an expensive tent at midnight is unusual and almost always honest. Treat the score as a reason to look, not as a verdict: in the project most flagged payments are legitimate even for the best model. Automatic blocking on an anomaly score alone punishes unusual customers, which is also why the project ends with a check of who receives the false alarms.

🛠️ Step 1: Load the payments and split by time (5 min)

Fraud patterns change over time, so test the way you will deploy: learn from the past, score the future. Never shuffle time-ordered payments into a random split.

🛠️ Step 2: A reporting helper and two rule baselines (10 min)

Write one function that reports everything you need for any score, then try two one-column rules: "largest amounts first" and "most orders in the last 24 hours first".

results = {}


def review_report(name, anomaly_score):
    """Rank June by score (higher = more suspicious) and report the queue."""
    anomaly_score = np.asarray(anomaly_score)
    queue = np.argsort(-anomaly_score)[:budget]
    results[name] = {
        "roc_auc": roc_auc_score(y_test, anomaly_score),
        "avg_precision": average_precision_score(y_test, anomaly_score),
        f"caught_in_top_{budget}": int(y_test[queue].sum()),
    }
    print(f"{name:20s} AUC {results[name]['roc_auc']:.3f}  AP {results[name]['avg_precision']:.3f}  "
          f"caught {y_test[queue].sum()} of {y_test.sum()}")


review_report("amount rule", test["amount"])
review_report("velocity rule", test["velocity_24h"] + test["amount"] / 1e6)   # amount only breaks ties

Output

amount rule          AUC 0.726  AP 0.038  caught 8 of 64
velocity rule        AUC 0.830  AP 0.250  caught 26 of 64

The amount rule catches only 8 of 64: big orders are mostly honest. Velocity is much better (26), because a burst of orders from one account is a strong collective-anomaly signal that someone already turned into a feature. These are the numbers to beat.

🛠️ Step 3: Isolation Forest without labels (10 min)

Build a pipeline that scales the numeric columns, one-hot encodes the category, and fits an Isolation Forest on January to May. It is given the features only, never is_fraud.

from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest

numeric = ["account_age_days", "amount", "hour", "items", "device_new", "billing_shipping_mismatch",
           "country_mismatch", "card_attempts", "velocity_24h"]


def make_preprocessor():
    return ColumnTransformer([
        ("num", StandardScaler(), numeric),
        ("cat", OneHotEncoder(sparse_output=False, handle_unknown="ignore"), ["category"]),
    ])


iso = make_pipeline(make_preprocessor(), IsolationForest(n_estimators=300, random_state=42))
iso.fit(train[numeric + ["category"]])
iso_score = -iso.score_samples(test)          # negate: higher = more anomalous
review_report("isolation forest", iso_score)

Output

isolation forest     AUC 0.848  AP 0.376  caught 32 of 64

Without seeing a single label, Isolation Forest catches 32 of June's 64 frauds in a queue of 165: about one review in five finds a fraud, against roughly one in 130 for a random queue. Its average precision of about 0.38 is more than 40 times the 0.008 a random score would get.

🛠️ Step 4: Compare LOF and a Gaussian mixture (10 min)

Try the two other detectors on the same split: LOF in novelty mode (it scores new data), and a five-component Gaussian mixture on the scaled numeric features. LOF needs nearest neighbors, so this takes several seconds.

from sklearn.neighbors import LocalOutlierFactor
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture

lof = make_pipeline(make_preprocessor(), LocalOutlierFactor(n_neighbors=50, novelty=True))
lof.fit(train[numeric + ["category"]])
review_report("local outlier factor", -lof.score_samples(test))

gmm = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), GaussianMixture(n_components=5, random_state=42))
gmm.fit(train[numeric])
review_report("gaussian mixture", -gmm.score_samples(test[numeric]))

Output

local outlier factor AUC 0.757  AP 0.105  caught 16 of 64
gaussian mixture     AUC 0.828  AP 0.177  caught 24 of 64

LOF is the weakest detector here, well behind even the one-column velocity rule. It looks for points that are sparse compared with their neighbors, but a fraud's nearest neighbors are often other odd payments (night-time, new devices), so locally it does not stand out. The mixture lands between LOF and Isolation Forest. No detector is best everywhere: the toy data favored LOF, these payments favor Isolation Forest. That is why you compare several on held-out data.

🛠️ Step 5: What do labels add? (10 min)

🛠️ Step 6: Choose the budget from costs, and check who is flagged (10 min)

for name, score in [("isolation forest", iso_score), ("supervised boosting", hgb_score)]:
    order = np.argsort(-score)
    for share in [0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05]:
        n = round(share * len(test))
        caught = y_test[order[:n]].sum()
        cost = REVIEW_COST * n + FRAUD_LOSS * (n_frauds - caught)
        print(f"{name:20s} review {share:5.1%} ({n:3d}): caught {caught:2d}, total cost ${cost:,}")

new_account = test["account_age_days"].to_numpy() < 30
honest = ~fraud
for name, in_queue in [("isolation forest", in_iso_queue), ("supervised boosting", in_hgb_queue)]:
    print(f"{name:20s} honest new accounts flagged {in_queue[honest & new_account].mean():.1%}, "
          f"honest established accounts flagged {in_queue[honest & ~new_account].mean():.1%}")

Reviewing nothing would cost $24,000 in June. Every queue beats that, and the label-free Isolation Forest roughly halves the loss at a 1% budget. Because a missed fraud costs 37 times a review, the cost curve is flat over a wide range of budgets: for Isolation Forest, 1%, 2% and 5% all land between about $13,100 and $13,700, so the choice can rest on what the team can staff. The supervised queue is cheapest at the 2% budget, $10,275, essentially the same as the $10,285 that Lesson 2.5's cost-tuned logistic regression reached on the same June payments. Labels are worth about $3,400 a month here; the detector is what you run before you have them, and alongside them for new patterns.

The fairness check tells a second story. The supervised model flags honest new customers about twice as often as established ones, because new accounts are over-represented among past frauds. That is not illegal discrimination, but it is friction for exactly the customers Pinecrest is trying to win, and it would be worse if account age were tied to a protected group. Reasonable responses: a lighter-touch review for new accounts (an email confirmation rather than a held order), monitoring the flag rate by group every month, and never auto-declining on the score alone.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

If I have labels, why bother with unsupervised detection?

Three reasons. Labels arrive late (a chargeback can take weeks), so a detector protects you in the meantime. Labels only describe the frauds someone caught, so a new scheme is invisible to a classifier until it has been labeled. And at the start of a project you may have no labels at all. In the project the classifier won on known fraud, which is the usual result; the detector still caught some frauds the classifier missed.

How is this different from the imbalanced-classes lesson?

Lesson 2.5 trained classifiers on the same payments using the labels, with class weights, resampling and threshold choice. This lesson asks what you can do without labels, and uses the labels only to judge the result. The two meet in Step 5 of the project, and in real systems they run side by side.

Should I scale features for Isolation Forest?

It does not need it: each cut is chosen within one feature's own range, so the units do not matter. The project scales anyway because the same preprocessor feeds LOF, which does need scaling, and scaling does no harm to the forest. Distance-based methods (LOF, k-nearest neighbors, One-Class SVM) always need scaled features.

What about anomalies in time series, like a sudden drop in a store's daily sales?

The same ideas apply once you turn time into features: compare each day with a forecast or with the same weekday in recent weeks, and score the size of the surprise (the residual). Large residuals are the anomalies. Lesson 10.4 builds forecasts for Pinecrest's daily store sales that you could use this way.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Anomaly detection learns what normal looks like and scores how far each case is from it; it needs no labels to train
  • Point, contextual and collective anomalies differ; good features (per-category amounts, order velocity) turn the last two into the first
  • z-score and IQR rules are transparent baselines but see one column at a time
  • Isolation Forest scores how quickly random cuts isolate a point; LOF compares a point's density with its neighbors'; a Gaussian mixture scores log-density
  • Detectors return -1 for outliers, and score_samples is higher for normal points: negate it to get an anomaly score
  • contamination only sets a threshold; choose the threshold from a review budget and costs
  • Judge detectors with ROC AUC, average precision and catches within the budget, against a rule baseline, on a time-based split
  • When labels exist, a supervised model usually wins, and the detector stays useful for new patterns; always check who receives the false alarms
