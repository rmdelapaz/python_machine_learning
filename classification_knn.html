🧭 Ask the Lookalikes

Every model so far has learned weights: numbers that summarize the training data, after which the data itself could be thrown away. k-nearest neighbors (kNN) takes the opposite approach. It keeps every training row, and when a new customer arrives it looks for the k past customers most similar to them. If 9 of their 25 closest lookalikes churned, it predicts a churn probability of 9/25 = 0.36.

💡 Mental Model: The Real-Estate Agent's Comps

Ask an experienced agent what a house will sell for and they will not reach for a formula. They pull up "comps": the five or six recently sold homes most like this one, with similar size, age and neighborhood, and average their prices. That is kNN. Two questions decide whether the estimate is any good: what counts as similar (is one extra bedroom a bigger difference than ten years of age?) and how many comps to use (one comp is a gamble; fifty comps drag in houses that are not really alike). Those are exactly the two dials of this lesson: the distance and k.

graph TD A["New customer"] --> B["Scale the features"] B --> C["Distance to every training row"] C --> D["Keep the k closest"] D --> E["Share of them who churned"]

Because nothing is learned in advance, kNN is called a lazy learner: fit just stores the training table (in a structure that makes searching fast), and all the work happens at predict time.

🤔 Why This Matters

kNN is rarely the most accurate model on a big table, but the idea at its heart, distance as similarity, runs through the rest of the course. Clustering (Module 8) groups customers by distance, anomaly detection flags rows far from everyone, recommendation systems (Lesson 11.2) find similar products, and KNNImputer fills gaps from lookalikes. kNN also has a rare strength: every prediction comes with real examples you can show to a person. "These 25 customers looked like her, and 9 of them left" is an explanation a retention team understands immediately.

📏 Measuring "Similar"

Start with the churn table and the ten numeric features from Lesson 1.1, split exactly as before.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

customers = pd.read_csv("data/customers_churn.csv")
features = ["tenure_months", "orders_12m", "spend_12m", "avg_order_value", "days_since_last_order",
            "returns_12m", "return_rate", "support_tickets_12m", "discount_share", "app_user"]
X = customers[features]
y = customers["churned"]
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=42)
print(X_train.shape, X_test.shape)

Output

(4500, 10) (1500, 10)

The everyday distance between two points is the straight-line, or Euclidean, distance: subtract feature by feature, square the differences, add them up and take the square root.

Euclidean distance = √( (a₁ − b₁)² + (a₂ − b₂)² + ... + (aₚ − bₚ)² )

Compute it by hand for the first two training customers, and look at where the distance comes from:

a, b = X_train.iloc[0], X_train.iloc[1]
diff = a - b
parts = pd.DataFrame({"customer A": a, "customer B": b, "squared diff": diff ** 2})
parts["share of total"] = parts["squared diff"] / parts["squared diff"].sum()
print(parts.round(3))
print(f"Euclidean distance: {np.sqrt((diff ** 2).sum()):.2f}")

Output

orders_12m                  2.000      10.000        64.000           0.001
spend_12m                 630.300     578.820      2650.190           0.037
avg_order_value           315.150      57.880     66187.853           0.935
days_since_last_order      56.000      56.000         0.000           0.000
returns_12m                 1.000       0.000         1.000           0.000

Euclidean distance: 266.00

94% of the distance comes from one column, avg_order_value, where the two customers differ by about $257. Yet look at what else differs: customer A joined one month ago and placed 2 orders without the app; customer B has been with Pinecrest for 44 months, placed 10 orders and uses the app. Those differences are measured in months, orders and 0/1 flags, so next to hundreds of dollars they barely register. As far as this distance is concerned, "similar customers" means "customers with about the same order value", and almost nothing else.

A second common choice is the Manhattan distance: add up the absolute differences, like walking a city grid instead of flying straight. It is a little less dominated by one large difference, but it has the same units problem. In scikit-learn both are the Minkowski distance with a power p: p=2 is Euclidean (the default) and p=1 is Manhattan.

⚖️ Scaling Decides Everything

For Ridge in Lesson 3.2, scaling made the penalty fair. For kNN it is more fundamental: the scaled features are the definition of similarity. After StandardScaler, a difference of one standard deviation in recency counts the same as one standard deviation in spend.

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

scaler = StandardScaler().set_output(transform="pandas").fit(X_train)
a_s, b_s = scaler.transform(X_train.iloc[[0, 1]]).to_numpy()
share = pd.Series((a_s - b_s) ** 2, index=features)
print((share / share.sum()).round(3).sort_values(ascending=False).head(4))
print(f"Euclidean distance (scaled): {np.sqrt(((a_s - b_s) ** 2).sum()):.2f}")

Output

avg_order_value    0.306
orders_12m         0.301
app_user           0.205
tenure_months      0.097
dtype: float64
Euclidean distance (scaled): 4.52

Now order count, order value, the app and tenure all have a say, which matches what a person would notice first: a brand-new customer with two big orders versus a long-standing app user with ten small ones. Does it matter for predictions? Compare the same model with and without scaling, judged by ROC AUC (Lesson 1.4), because the goal is ranking customers by risk:

from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

for name, model in [("unscaled", KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=15)),
                    ("scaled", make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=15)))]:
    model.fit(X_train, y_train)
    auc = roc_auc_score(y_test, model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1])
    print(f"{name:9s} accuracy {model.score(X_test, y_test):.3f}   ROC AUC {auc:.3f}")

Output

unscaled  accuracy 0.851   ROC AUC 0.774
scaled    accuracy 0.858   ROC AUC 0.836

Same algorithm, same k, same data: the AUC jumps from 0.774 to 0.836 just by making the features comparable. (Accuracy barely moves, a reminder from Lesson 1.4 that accuracy is a blunt tool when only one customer in six churns.)

⚠️ Scaling Is a Modeling Choice, Not a Formality

Standard scaling says "every feature matters equally to begin with". That is a reasonable default, not a law. MinMaxScaler gives slightly different neighbors; dropping a feature removes it from the notion of similarity entirely; a binary column like app_user becomes a big jump of about two standard deviations. Try alternatives with cross-validation instead of assuming one is right. And as always, fit the scaler on training data only, which a pipeline guarantees.

🚀 A First kNN Model

Build the scaled model as a pipeline and look inside one prediction. predict_proba for kNN is simply the share of the k neighbors in each class.

knn = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=15)).fit(X_train, y_train)

proba = knn.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
print("first five churn probabilities:", proba[:5].round(3))
print("as counts out of 15:", (proba[:5] * 15).round().astype(int))
print("distinct probability values:", np.unique(proba).size)

Output

first five churn probabilities: [0.133 0.467 0.133 0.067 0.133]
as counts out of 15: [2 7 2 1 2]
distinct probability values: 15

With 15 neighbors there are only 16 possible probabilities: 0/15, 1/15, ..., 15/15 (15 of them occur in this test set). That coarseness matters later. Now ask the model who the neighbors are. kneighbors lives on the classifier, so first transform the customer with the pipeline's scaler (knn[:-1] is every step except the last):

riskiest = int(np.argmax(proba))
customer = X_test.iloc[[riskiest]]
distances, positions = knn[-1].kneighbors(knn[:-1].transform(customer))

lookalikes = X_train.iloc[positions[0]].assign(churned=y_train.iloc[positions[0]].to_numpy(),
                                              distance=distances[0].round(2))
print(customer[["tenure_months", "days_since_last_order", "discount_share", "support_tickets_12m", "app_user"]])
print(lookalikes[["days_since_last_order", "discount_share", "app_user", "churned", "distance"]].head(6))
print(f"lookalikes who churned: {lookalikes['churned'].sum()} of 15 -> probability {proba[riskiest]:.3f}")

Output

This is kNN's party trick. The prediction is not an opaque score; it is a list of real past customers who look like this one, along with what happened to them. You can see what they share: a long gap since the last order and no app. (A caveat for later: showing someone's lookalikes means showing other customers' data. In a real system you would summarize them, not print them, which is what the project does.)

🎚️ Choosing k

k is kNN's flexibility dial, and it works backwards from what you might expect. With k=1, every prediction copies a single neighbor: the model can draw a boundary around every individual training customer. That is maximum flexibility and maximum variance. With a huge k, every prediction averages so many customers that it drifts toward the overall churn rate: high bias. Watch the training and test scores:

for k in [1, 5, 15, 51, 151, 501]:
    m = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k)).fit(X_train, y_train)
    train_auc = roc_auc_score(y_train, m.predict_proba(X_train)[:, 1])
    test_auc = roc_auc_score(y_test, m.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1])
    print(f"k={k:3d}   train AUC {train_auc:.3f}   test AUC {test_auc:.3f}")

Output

k=  1   train AUC 1.000   test AUC 0.645
k=  5   train AUC 0.932   test AUC 0.789
k= 15   train AUC 0.893   test AUC 0.836
k= 51   train AUC 0.865   test AUC 0.842
k=151   train AUC 0.848   test AUC 0.837
k=501   train AUC 0.833   test AUC 0.823

At k=1 the training AUC is a perfect 1.000, because every training customer's nearest neighbor is themselves. That number is meaningless, and the test AUC shows it. The test AUC climbs quickly, levels off somewhere around 50 to 150 neighbors, and slowly sags after that: the bias-variance U from Lesson 3.2, upside down because higher AUC is better. To see what the dial does, use just two features so the model can be drawn:

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.inspection import DecisionBoundaryDisplay

two = ["days_since_last_order", "discount_share"]

        m, X_train[two], response_method="predict_proba", grid_resolution=150, eps=0,
        ax=ax, cmap="RdBu_r", levels=np.linspace(0, 1, 11), alpha=0.8)
    ax.scatter(shown[two[0]], shown[two[1]], c=y_train.loc[shown.index], cmap="RdBu_r",
               edgecolor="black", linewidth=0.3, s=10)
    auc = roc_auc_score(y_test, m.predict_proba(X_test[two])[:, 1])
    ax.set_title(f"k = {k}   (test AUC {auc:.3f})")
    ax.set_xlabel("days since last order")
    ax.set_ylabel("")

The k=1 map is a patchwork: one churner deep in loyal territory gets their own red island. With k=25 the real pattern appears: churn risk rises sharply once a customer has been away for about 150 days, and rises with discount reliance. With k=301 the picture is smooth. Notice that kNN found the 150-day threshold without being told it exists, something a straight-line model cannot express.

🔁 Choose k with Cross-Validation

As with alpha in Lesson 3.2, pick k on the training data only. StratifiedKFold keeps the churn rate the same in every fold:

from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score

cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
for k in [5, 15, 25, 51, 101, 151]:
    scores = cross_val_score(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k)),
                             X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring="roc_auc")
    print(f"k={k:3d}   CV AUC {scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}")

Output

k=  5   CV AUC 0.778 ± 0.012
k= 15   CV AUC 0.826 ± 0.016
k= 25   CV AUC 0.834 ± 0.023
k= 51   CV AUC 0.839 ± 0.018
k=101   CV AUC 0.838 ± 0.021
k=151   CV AUC 0.835 ± 0.021

Cross-validation points to about 51 neighbors, and the plateau is flat: anything from 25 to 151 is within one standard deviation. When a plateau is that flat, prefer the middle of it; you are choosing a range, not a magic number.

✅ A Surprise Worth Noticing

Tuned kNN reaches a test AUC of about 0.84 on these ten features, ahead of logistic regression's 0.823 on the same columns (the noise-column experiment below measures both). kNN has no idea what a threshold or an interaction is, but by asking lookalikes it can pick up both kinds of pattern, such as the 150-day recency cliff in the figure, which a weighted sum of features cannot draw. Simple local averaging is a surprisingly strong baseline whenever effects are nonlinear and the number of features is small.

🌌 The Curse of Dimensionality

kNN has a weakness that grows with every column you add. In a few dimensions, "nearest" means something: your 25 closest lookalikes really are much closer than a random customer. In many dimensions, almost every point is about equally far from every other, and "nearest" stops meaning "similar". You can measure this with random points: compare each point's nearest distance with its farthest.

from sklearn.metrics import pairwise_distances

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
for n_dims in [2, 10, 100, 1000]:
    points = rng.uniform(size=(1000, n_dims))
    d = pairwise_distances(points[:1], points[1:])[0]
    print(f"{n_dims:4d} dimensions: nearest / farthest distance = {d.min() / d.max():.3f}")

Output

   2 dimensions: nearest / farthest distance = 0.010
  10 dimensions: nearest / farthest distance = 0.317
 100 dimensions: nearest / farthest distance = 0.715
1000 dimensions: nearest / farthest distance = 0.890

In 2 dimensions the nearest point is a hundred times closer than the farthest. In 1,000 dimensions it is only about 10% closer: the neighborhood has dissolved. Real data is rarely this bad, because real features are correlated and the data lies near a lower-dimensional surface. But every irrelevant feature adds noise to every distance. Test it: add columns of pure random noise to the churn features and compare kNN with logistic regression.

from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

for n_noise in [0, 5, 20, 50]:
    noise = pd.DataFrame(rng.normal(size=(len(X), n_noise)), index=X.index).add_prefix("noise_")
    X_noisy = pd.concat([X, noise], axis=1)
    Xn_train, Xn_test = X_noisy.loc[X_train.index], X_noisy.loc[X_test.index]
    aucs = []
    for clf in [KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=51), LogisticRegression()]:
        m = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), clf).fit(Xn_train, y_train)
        aucs.append(roc_auc_score(y_test, m.predict_proba(Xn_test)[:, 1]))
    print(f"{n_noise:2d} noise columns   kNN AUC {aucs[0]:.3f}   logistic AUC {aucs[1]:.3f}")

Output

 0 noise columns   kNN AUC 0.842   logistic AUC 0.823
 5 noise columns   kNN AUC 0.823   logistic AUC 0.824
20 noise columns   kNN AUC 0.790   logistic AUC 0.820
50 noise columns   kNN AUC 0.755   logistic AUC 0.816

Logistic regression learns a weight for each column and simply gives the noise weights near zero, so it barely notices. kNN has no weights: after scaling, every column has an equal say in the distance, so 50 noise columns drown the 10 real ones and the AUC falls to about 0.75. For kNN, feature selection is part of the model. The same logic applies to redundant features: spend_12m is almost exactly orders_12m times avg_order_value, so spending effectively gets counted twice in every distance. The project tests a smaller feature set for exactly this reason.

🧰 kNN in Practice

A few settings and facts round out the picture:

SettingWhat it doesWhen to change it
n_neighborsHow many lookalikes vote (default 5, usually too few)Always tune it by cross-validation
weights="distance"Closer neighbors get a bigger vote (weight 1/distance)Try it; often a small gain with a large k
p=1 or metric=...Manhattan instead of Euclidean, or another metric such as "cosine"Try p=1; cosine for text and ratings (Lesson 11.2)
algorithmHow neighbors are searched: "auto", "kd_tree", "ball_tree", "brute"Rarely; "auto" picks well. Results are the same, only speed differs

Speed. Training is nearly free and prediction is the expensive part, the reverse of most models. Every prediction searches the stored training rows, so prediction time grows with the size of the training set, and the whole training set must ship with the model. Time it on your machine:

import time

for name, model in [("kNN, k=51", make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=51))),
                    ("logistic regression", make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression()))]:
    start = time.perf_counter()
    model.fit(X_train, y_train)
    fit_ms = (time.perf_counter() - start) * 1000
    start = time.perf_counter()
    model.predict_proba(X_test)
    predict_ms = (time.perf_counter() - start) * 1000
    print(f"{name:20s} fit {fit_ms:6.1f} ms   predict 1,500 rows {predict_ms:6.1f} ms")

Output (your numbers will differ)

kNN, k=51            fit   21.2 ms   predict 1,500 rows  232.9 ms
logistic regression  fit  206.4 ms   predict 1,500 rows    1.2 ms

For thousands of rows both are instant. For millions of rows and many queries per second, kNN needs approximate nearest-neighbor indexes, a topic for production systems rather than this course.

Regression too. KNeighborsRegressor predicts the average target of the k neighbors instead of a vote. Exercise 3 uses it to forecast snowshoe sales from the weather.

💡 Where You Will Meet Neighbors Again

sklearn.neighbors.NearestNeighbors is kNN without the prediction: just "find the closest rows". K-Means (Lesson 8.1) assigns each customer to the nearest cluster center; DBSCAN (Lesson 8.4) defines clusters by how many neighbors lie within a radius; item-based recommendations (Lesson 11.2) find the products nearest to the ones a customer bought. Everything you learned here about scaling and dimensions carries over directly.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

Forgetting to scale is the classic kNN mistake, and you have already seen its cost (AUC 0.774 instead of 0.836). Here are four more.

1. Asking for More Neighbors Than Rows

tiny_train = X_train.head(10)
KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=15).fit(tiny_train, y_train.head(10)).predict(X_test.head(3))

Error

ValueError: Expected n_neighbors <= n_samples_fit, but n_neighbors = 15, n_samples_fit = 10, n_samples = 3

What it means: fit happily stored 10 rows, and predict then failed because it cannot find 15 neighbors among them. It bites when you tune k on small groups or cross-validation folds. Fix: keep k well below the number of training rows in the smallest fold.

2. Missing Values in the Features

with_gaps = customers[["days_since_last_order", "email_opens_90d"]]
KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=15).fit(with_gaps, y)

Error

ValueError: Input X contains NaN.
KNeighborsClassifier does not accept missing values encoded as NaN natively. For supervised learning, you might want to consider sklearn.ensemble.HistGradientBoostingClassifier and Regressor which accept missing values encoded as NaNs natively. Alternatively, it is possible to preprocess the data, for instance by using an imputer transformer in a pipeline or drop samples with missing values. See https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/impute.html You can find a list of all estimators that handle NaN values at the following page: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/impute.html#estimators-that-handle-nan-values

What it means: a distance cannot be computed when one of the numbers is missing. Fix: impute inside the pipeline (Lesson 2.1), for example make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median"), StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(...)). KNNImputer fills each gap from the row's nearest neighbors, the same idea used for filling instead of predicting.

3. Ranking With a Tiny k

knn5 = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5)).fit(X_train, y_train)
p5 = knn5.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
print("distinct probabilities:", np.unique(p5))
print("customers tied at the top value:", (p5 == p5.max()).sum())

Output (no error, and that is the problem)

distinct probabilities: [0.  0.2 0.4 0.6 0.8 1. ]
customers tied at the top value: 22

What happened: with k=5 every probability is 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 or 1.0. Twenty-two customers tie at 1.0, so a top-50 shortlist is partly decided by row order, not by risk, and the AUC suffers because ties cannot be ranked. Fix: use a larger k (chosen by cross-validation) or weights="distance", both of which spread the probabilities out.

4. Scaling Before Cross-Validation

X_train_scaled_all = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X_train)   # fitted on every fold at once
leaky = cross_val_score(KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=51), X_train_scaled_all, y_train, cv=cv, scoring="roc_auc")
honest = cross_val_score(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=51)),
                         X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring="roc_auc")
print(f"scaled outside the folds {leaky.mean():.4f}   pipeline {honest.mean():.4f}")

Output (no error, and that is the problem)

scaled outside the folds 0.8390   pipeline 0.8391

What happened: the scaler saw each validation fold's rows before that fold was scored, so the scores are not a fair simulation of new data. Here the difference is tiny because means and standard deviations of 4,500 customers are stable; with smaller data, or with imputation and feature selection done outside the folds, the leak can be large. Fix: put every preprocessing step in the pipeline and cross-validate the whole pipeline (Lesson 2.2).

🛠️ Step 3: Choose features and k by cross-validation (10 min)

Compare all ten features with a leaner set that drops the redundant spending columns and the returns columns, for several values of k and both vote weightings. Only the training rows are used.

from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

lean_features = ["tenure_months", "orders_12m", "days_since_last_order",
                 "support_tickets_12m", "discount_share", "app_user"]
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)

rows = []
for set_name, cols in [("all 10", all_features), ("lean 6", lean_features)]:
    for k in [25, 51, 101]:
        for weights in ["uniform", "distance"]:
            model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k, weights=weights))
            scores = cross_val_score(model, train[cols], y_train, cv=cv, scoring="roc_auc")
            rows.append({"features": set_name, "k": k, "weights": weights, "cv_auc": scores.mean()})
search = pd.DataFrame(rows).sort_values("cv_auc", ascending=False)
print(search.head(5).round(4).to_string(index=False))

🛠️ Step 4: Fit the winner and test once (5 min)

best = search.iloc[0]
chosen_cols = lean_features if best["features"] == "lean 6" else all_features

The test AUC lands right on the cross-validated estimate, which is the sign of an honest process, and clearly ahead of logistic regression's 0.823. The top 50 tells a more modest story: 31 real churners, against 33 for logistic regression and 41 for the random forest in Lesson 1.1. AUC grades the whole ranking of 1,500 customers; the top 50 is a thin slice of it. If the team will only ever call 50 people, that slice is the number to optimize (Lesson 1.4's precision at a cutoff), and a better AUC does not guarantee it.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Should I use an odd k to avoid ties?

For a two-class vote with uniform weights, an odd k avoids 50/50 ties in predict, which is why this lesson uses 15, 25, 51 and 101. It matters little in practice: with a large k exact ties are rare, weights="distance" almost never ties, and when you rank customers by predict_proba the vote threshold does not matter at all.

How do I use category columns like home_store in kNN?

One-hot encode them (Lesson 2.1) and scale the result along with everything else, inside a ColumnTransformer. Be aware that each category adds columns, and a mismatch on a one-hot column counts as a big jump in distance. Try with and without the categories and let cross-validation decide; for many categorical columns, tree models (Module 5) are usually the better tool.

Is showing a customer's lookalikes a privacy problem?

It can be. The explanation for one person's prediction is literally other people's records. Inside the company, a summary ("12 of 51 similar customers left; they had been away about 200 days") is usually enough and safer than raw rows. Never expose neighbors to customers themselves, and avoid using sensitive attributes in the distance at all: if the neighbors are chosen partly by age group, the model treats people differently by age, which Lesson 7.3 shows how to check.

When should I actually pick kNN for a project?

When the number of useful features is small (roughly under 20), the training set fits in memory, and you value explanations by example. It is also a quick, assumption-light baseline for any tabular problem. With many features, very large data, or a need for fast predictions, gradient boosting (Lesson 5.3) or a linear model is usually the better choice.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • kNN stores the training rows and predicts from the k most similar ones: a vote share for classification, an average for regression
  • Distance defines similarity; Euclidean (p=2) and Manhattan (p=1) are the common choices
  • Unscaled features let the largest units dominate every distance; scaling in a pipeline raised churn AUC from 0.774 to 0.836
  • k is the flexibility dial: small k means high variance, large k high bias; choose it by cross-validation
  • Irrelevant and redundant features blur distances (the curse of dimensionality), so feature selection is part of a kNN model
  • kNN trains instantly, predicts slowly, carries its training data, and explains predictions with real examples
