🔭 Going Further: Genetic Algorithms

Genetic algorithms search for good solutions by imitating evolution: keep the fitter candidates, mix them, mutate them, repeat. They are simple and flexible, and in machine learning they are a niche tool rather than a default.

🧬 The Idea

Most of the optimizers in this course follow a gradient: they compute which direction makes the loss smaller and take a step that way. That needs a smooth, differentiable objective. Many real problems do not have one. Which 12 of 40 features to keep, which order to visit 30 stores in, which combination of settings for a pipeline: these are choices among discrete options, the objective may be noisy, and there is no slope to follow.

A genetic algorithm (GA) needs only a way to score a candidate. It keeps a population of candidate solutions, each encoded as a list of values (a chromosome), and repeats four steps:

graph TD A["Random population"] --> B["Score each one: fitness"] B --> C["Selection: favor the fitter"] C --> D["Crossover: mix two parents"] D --> E["Mutation: small random changes"] E -->|"next generation"| B

💡 Mental Model: Breeding Trail Routes

Imagine planning hiking routes by committee. You sketch fifty random routes and time each one (fitness). The faster routes get to "have children": a new route takes the first half of one good route and the second half of another (crossover), and now and then a random detour is swapped (mutation). After many rounds the population is full of fast routes, without anyone having worked out the best route mathematically. Nothing guarantees the very best one is found; you usually find a good one.

Each operator has many variants. Selection: tournament selection (pick a few at random, keep the best) is simple and robust; roulette-wheel selection picks in proportion to fitness. Crossover: one-point, two-point or uniform (each gene from a random parent). Mutation: flip a bit, or add a little Gaussian noise to a number. Elitism copies the best individual unchanged into the next generation, so the best score never gets worse.

🧪 A Genetic Algorithm in 25 Lines

The test problem is the Rastrigin function, a standard benchmark for optimizers: a bowl covered in a regular grid of bumps, so it has a huge number of local minima, and one global minimum of 0 at the origin. Gradient descent started at a random point gets stuck in the nearest dip. Here it has five dimensions.

import numpy as np


def rastrigin(x):
    """Many local minima; the global minimum is 0 at (0, 0, ..., 0). Works on one point or a whole population."""
    return 10 * x.shape[-1] + (x**2 - 10 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * x)).sum(axis=-1)


def genetic_algorithm(f, dim=5, pop_size=60, generations=80, mutation_sd=0.3, seed=42):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    pop = rng.uniform(-5.12, 5.12, size=(pop_size, dim))
    history = []
    for _ in range(generations):
        fitness = f(pop)                                          # lower is better here
        history.append(fitness.min())
        a, b = rng.integers(pop_size, size=(2, pop_size))         # tournament selection
        parents = np.where((fitness[a] < fitness[b])[:, None], pop[a], pop[b])
        mask = rng.random((pop_size, dim)) < 0.5                  # uniform crossover
        children = np.where(mask, parents, np.roll(parents, 1, axis=0))
        mutate = rng.random((pop_size, dim)) < 0.2                # Gaussian mutation
        children = np.clip(children + mutate * rng.normal(0, mutation_sd, (pop_size, dim)), -5.12, 5.12)
        children[0] = pop[fitness.argmin()]                       # elitism
        pop = children
    fitness = f(pop)
    return pop[fitness.argmin()], fitness.min(), history


best_x, best_f, history = genetic_algorithm(rastrigin)
print("best value found:", round(best_f, 3))
print("best point:", best_x.round(2))
print("best value every 10 generations:", np.round(history[::10], 2))

Output

best value found: 0.166
best point: [-0.01 -0.    0.01  0.01 -0.02]
best value every 10 generations: [44.63  7.97  3.33  1.84  1.19  1.1   1.1   0.32]

The best value falls from about 45 to under 0.2, and the best point sits within a few hundredths of the true answer at the origin. Is that impressive? Only a baseline can say. Random search with exactly the same number of function evaluations:

evaluations = 60 * 81                     # population x (generations + final scoring)
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
samples = rng.uniform(-5.12, 5.12, size=(evaluations, 5))
print(f"random search, {evaluations} evaluations: best value {rastrigin(samples).min():.3f}")

Output

random search, 4860 evaluations: best value 23.065

Random search, given the same budget, does far worse in five dimensions: the GA's selection and crossover concentrate the search where good solutions are. That is the honest case for evolutionary methods on rugged, many-dimensional objectives.

⚠️ Many Knobs, Few Guarantees

Population size, mutation rate, mutation size and the number of generations all change the result, and the best settings differ by problem. Change the seed and you get a different answer. A GA gives no proof that it found the optimum and no rule for when to stop. Always run it with several seeds and compare it with a simple baseline, exactly as you would a model.

📦 Use a Library When You Can

Writing a GA is a great way to understand one; maintaining your own is rarely worth it. SciPy ships differential evolution, a closely related evolutionary method for continuous problems that is well tested and needs almost no tuning:

from scipy.optimize import differential_evolution

result = differential_evolution(rastrigin, bounds=[(-5.12, 5.12)] * 5, rng=42)
print(f"best value {result.fun:.6f} at {result.x.round(4)} after {result.nfev} evaluations")

Output

best value 0.000000 at [ 0. -0.  0. -0. -0.] after 12156 evaluations

It reaches the exact optimum, at the cost of more evaluations than the hand-written GA used. For discrete or multi-objective problems, dedicated libraries such as DEAP and pymoo implement many GA variants, including NSGA-II, the standard algorithm for finding trade-off curves between two objectives (for example accuracy against model size).

🤝 Where GAs Meet Machine Learning

  • Feature selection. Encode a feature subset as a string of 0s and 1s and use cross-validated score as fitness. It works, but every fitness evaluation is a full cross-validation, so it is slow, and scoring thousands of subsets on the same folds invites overfitting the selection to those folds. Lesson 2.4's simpler methods (L1 regularization, RFECV and sequential selection, plus Lesson 7.1's permutation importance) are usually the better first choice. If you do use a GA, keep a test set it never sees.
  • Hyperparameter and pipeline search. TPOT evolves whole scikit-learn pipelines (see the AutoML reading). For expensive models with a handful of continuous hyperparameters, Bayesian optimization usually needs fewer evaluations; evolutionary methods shine with many, mixed, conditional choices.
  • Neuroevolution. Evolving network weights or architectures. Evolution strategies have been used as a parallel-friendly alternative to gradient methods in reinforcement learning, and evolutionary search has found competitive network architectures, but gradient descent remains the standard way to train networks.
  • Outside ML, in operations. Scheduling, routing and layout problems, where solutions are discrete and constraints are messy, are where GAs earn their keep. A delivery route for Pinecrest's four stores is a more natural fit than a churn model.

⚖️ An Honest Verdict

Genetic algorithms are general, easy to explain and easy to parallelize, because each candidate in a generation can be scored independently. They need no gradient and tolerate noise. In return, they are slow per unit of improvement, sensitive to their settings and come with no guarantees. When a gradient exists, use it. When evaluations are expensive and the space is small and continuous, try Bayesian optimization first. When the problem is discrete, constrained, multi-objective or simply strange, a GA or another evolutionary method is a reasonable, well-understood tool.

✅ Key Takeaways

  • A GA keeps a population of candidates and repeats selection, crossover and mutation, guided only by a fitness score
  • On a rugged test function it beats random search with the same budget, but it has many settings and no guarantee of the optimum
  • Prefer a tested library (SciPy's differential_evolution, DEAP, pymoo) over your own implementation
  • In ML, GAs appear in feature selection and pipeline search; watch the cost and the risk of overfitting the search
  • Gradient methods for differentiable problems, Bayesian optimization for small expensive searches, evolutionary methods for discrete and messy ones
