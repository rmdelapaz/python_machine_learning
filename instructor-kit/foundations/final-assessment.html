Final Project: A Supervised Model for Pinecrest

The end-of-course assessment of Machine Learning Foundations. Each learner chooses one of three supervised problems on the Pinecrest data (churn, weekly demand or fraud), builds one model end to end with the course's leak-safe split, a baseline, a fair comparison, honest tuning and one look at the test set, and reports the decision in one page. Score it with the seven-row rubric below. This page also holds the instructor answer notes with reference numbers for all three problems: it is for instructors only.

Covers Modules 1–6 Chosen Session 10, studio Session 11 Submit within 1 week Successful: 3+ on every row Instructor only

The Brief (as the Instructor Sees It)

Pinecrest's analytics lead, in the words used in Session 10: "Pick one of three problems we actually have. Build one supervised model end to end, the way this course taught: frame the decision, split the data the way the model will be used, beat a do-nothing baseline, compare candidates fairly, tune without fooling yourself, open the test set once, and tell us in one page what we should do and how sure you are. An honest 'the simple model is enough' is a perfectly good answer."

Weighting and the result
  • Score each row 1 to 4 (whole numbers). The weighted score is the sum of score × weight, out of 4.00. Weights: Framing and metric 10%, Leak-safe data and split 20%, Baselines 10%, Fair model comparison 15%, Tuning and honest estimates 15%, Test once and the decision 15%, Report and reproducibility 15%.
  • Successful final project: 3 (Proficient) or higher on every row. Report the weighted score alongside it.
  • Successful with a revision: one row at 2 and a weighted score of at least 2.75; the learner fixes that row within one week and you re-score that row only.
  • Not yet: any row at 1, two or more rows at 2, or a notebook that does not run. Return written feedback (one thing done well per row and the single change that would most improve the work) and allow one resubmission within two weeks.
  • Leakage overrides polish: a model trained with a leaking column or a split that mixes the test period into training scores at most 2 on "Leak-safe data and split" AND on "Test once and the decision", however good the numbers look.
  • Reproducibility first: rerun the notebook before reading anything else. If it fails, ask for a fix within 48 hours before scoring the other rows.

Answer Notes · Problem A: Churn

A sound analysis: steps
  • The 11 numeric and 5 categorical features of Module 2 (cancellation_reason out), the canonical split, preprocessing inside pipelines (median / most-frequent impute, scale, one-hot; or native categories for HistGradientBoosting).
  • Bar to beat: random-order AP 0.165 (= the test churn rate), offering to everyone, and offering to nobody ($0).
  • Candidates on one StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=42) (or the 6.1 repeated splitter), deciding metric average precision (the care team works from the top of a ranked list); a paired check of the top two.
  • A random search over boosting (the 6.2 space: loguniform rates and L2, randint leaf counts and leaf sizes, 10 to 15 settings) on the training rows.
  • The threshold for the $15 / $60 economics chosen on the training rows (TunedThresholdClassifierCV with a profit scorer, or an out-of-fold profit curve, Lesson 1.4); the test set opened once for the chosen model and threshold.
Key numbers (from the instructor's own run on the course data)
  • 5-fold CV average precision on the training rows: logistic regression 0.528 ± 0.042 (AUC 0.832), random forest (200 trees, leaf 5) 0.619 ± 0.042, default HistGradientBoosting 0.608 ± 0.043 (AUC 0.861), baseline 0.165.
  • Tuned boosting (15 random settings, AP): best_score_ 0.636 with learning_rate 0.0472, max_leaf_nodes 50, min_samples_leaf 199, l2 0.0292. Test, once: AP 0.614, ROC AUC 0.873 (default: 0.6 / 0.86).
  • Churners in the top 150 test customers (the care team's quarterly calls): logistic 87, forest 97, default boosting 101, tuned boosting 100; a random list reaches about 25. Tuning changes the ranking quality a little and the calls not at all.
  • Logistic regression with four shape features (lapsed > 150 days, tenure < 6 months, tenure > 72 months, tickets × no app): CV AP 0.643 (AUC 0.878), test AP 0.621 (AUC 0.874): as good as tuned boosting, from features alone.
  • Threshold chosen on the training rows for the tuned booster: 0.222 (break-even 0.25). Test, once: 395 offers, 186 reach a churner, profit $5,235; the same model at 0.5 earns $3,780, at a fixed 0.25 $5,400; offering to everyone -$7,620. Logistic regression with its own tuned threshold (0.233): $4,080 with 352 offers.
The answer the data support

Offer the retention deal to customers whose predicted churn probability is above about 0.22 to 0.25: on 1,500 held-out customers that is about 395 offers reaching about 186 real churners, an expected profit of about $5,235 against a loss of $7,620 for offering to everyone and $3,780 at the default 0.5. Most of the gain over the default comes from the threshold, not from tuning; a tuned booster and a feature-built logistic regression are equally good choices, and the simpler one is easier to explain. The dollar figures rest on the $15 and $60 estimates.

Traps (and what they report)
  • cancellation_reason in X: test ROC AUC 0.939, AP 0.866. Too good to be true: it is filled in only after a customer leaves.
  • Accuracy as the deciding metric: doing nothing scores 0.835; a model at 0.85 sounds weak and is useful, or sounds strong and is useless.
  • The threshold chosen on the test set: the best test threshold (0.27) shows $5,535, about $300 more than the honest $5,235; it is a best-of-many on the grading data, not a result.
  • best_score_ (0.636) reported as the model's performance: the one-time test AP is 0.614.
  • Several test looks: comparing every candidate on the test set and keeping the best. The leaderboard belongs to the training rows.
What an honest result sounds like
  • "Tuning raised cross-validated AP from 0.608 to 0.636 (best of 15 settings, so optimistic), and test AP from 0.600 to 0.614; in the care team's top 150 it made no difference (100 against 101 churners). The decision that matters is the threshold: chosen on the training customers at 0.22, it earns about $5,200 per 1,500 customers."
  • Follow-up questions: "Which rows chose your threshold?" · "How many more churners does your model put in the top 150 than the simplest model?" · "How would the plan change if an offer cost $20?"
  • Weak submissions: report accuracy; include cancellation_reason; quote best_score_ as performance; give a profit with no comparison to offering to everyone.

Answer Notes · Problem B: Weekly Demand

A sound analysis: steps
  • Features known when the 2025 plan is made: store, product, category (category dtype), week of year, list price, discount, price, promo, holiday week, temperature (as if a seasonal forecast were available: a caveat to state). Not the year, not any 2025 sales.
  • Train on 2024 (5,088 rows: 53 week starts, because 2024-12-30 belongs to 2024), test on 2025 (4,896).
  • Baselines: the 2024 overall mean, each product's 2024 mean, per product and store, the same week last year.
  • CV inside 2024 on blocks of weeks (KFold(5) without shuffling), MAE in units; a Poisson loss for counts (Lesson 5.3).
  • A random search on the blocks; the default kept if it is not beaten; 2025 opened once; MAE overall and per category; the total forecast against the total sold.
Key numbers (from the instructor's own run on the course data)
  • 2025 MAE of the simple rules: overall mean 17.13, per-product mean 13.42, per product and store 12.95, same week last year 13.81.
  • HistGradientBoostingRegressor, native categories: squared error 8.16, Poisson 8.05 (R² 0.744): 40% below the per-product mean.
  • Cross-validation inside 2024 for the Poisson model: shuffled KFold 7.6 ± 0.19 (flattering), blocks of weeks 8.49 ± 1.34, TimeSeriesSplit 9.77 ± 1.04 (early folds train on very little of the year).
  • A 15-setting random search on the blocks: best CV MAE 8.57 (WORSE than the default's 8.49); its 2025 MAE 8.22 against 8.05. Keep the default.
  • Per category (Poisson boosting, 2025, baseline in brackets): Snow 4.45 (baseline 13.33); Packs 5.5 (baseline 9.16); Climbing 5.71 (baseline 9.11); Sleeping 6.63 (baseline 10.69); Tents 6.82 (baseline 11.27); Apparel 8.08 (baseline 12.59); Accessories 8.86 (baseline 13.07); Camp Kitchen 11.61 (baseline 19.31); Footwear 12.85 (baseline 18.72).
  • Level: mean forecast 24.6 units per row against 26.3 sold in 2025 (24.4 in 2024): the total forecast is 121,456 units against 128,970 sold, about 6% low, because trees cannot extrapolate growth they never saw.
  • Zeros: 105 weeks sold 0 in 2025; 5 of them had a forecast above 12 units (likely stockouts, which a sales history records as zero demand).
The answer the data support

Default Poisson gradient boosting forecasts 2025 weekly units with an MAE of about 8.05 units against 13.42 for each product's 2024 average: the biggest gains are where the season matters (Snow 4.45 against 13.33). Tuning did not improve it. The forecast is about 6% low overall because 2025 grew; a buyer should scale it up or the model should be retrained as 2025 data arrive.

Traps (and what they report)
  • A shuffled 75/25 split over both years: MAE 7.36, better than the honest 8.05, because 2025 weeks leak into training.
  • Shuffled CV folds inside 2024: 7.6, which then makes every tuning comparison look rosier than 2025 will.
  • Last week's units as a feature: MAE 8.17 (no better here), and invalid for a plan made at the start of the year; valid only for a one-week-ahead forecaster, a different project.
  • year as a feature: MAE 8.05 (no effect: trees cannot extrapolate a year they never saw), and it hides the growth problem rather than solving it.
  • Reporting RMSE or R² only: the buyers think in units; MAE in units, by category, is the business measure.
What an honest result sounds like
  • "A search over 15 settings did not beat the default settings (CV MAE 8.57 against 8.49 on blocks of 2024 weeks), so we kept the default; on 2025 it is off by 8.05 units a week on average, 40% better than each product's 2024 average." That is a Proficient tuning row.
  • Follow-up questions: "On 2 January 2025, which of your features would you not know?" · "Why are your shuffled-fold scores better than your 2025 score?" · "Is the forecast too high or too low in total, and why?"
  • Weak submissions: a random split; R² only; a tuned model that is worse but reported because it was tuned; no per-category view.

Answer Notes · Problem C: Fraud

A sound analysis: steps
  • Nine numeric columns plus category; not transaction_id or timestamp (order, not behavior); train January to May, test June.
  • Bar to beat in June: review nothing, review everything; "always legitimate" accuracy shows why accuracy is useless here.
  • Candidates on stratified folds inside January to May, deciding metric average precision: logistic regression (scaled, one-hot), default and regularized HistGradientBoosting (Lesson 5.3 Exercise 4 settings), optionally a forest.
  • A random search over boosting with early stopping; then a review threshold for $10 / $375 chosen on January to May (TunedThresholdClassifierCV with a cost scorer; the formula gives 10 / 375 = 0.027 if probabilities are calibrated).
  • June opened once: queue size (per day), frauds caught, cost, against reviewing nothing.
Key numbers (from the instructor's own run on the course data)
  • 5-fold CV average precision on January to May: logistic 0.506 ± 0.054 (AUC 0.855), default boosting 0.443 ± 0.033 (overfits 374 frauds), regularized boosting 0.528 ± 0.048; baseline about 0.009.
  • Tuned boosting (15 settings, early stopping): best_score_ 0.529 (learning_rate 0.1011, max_leaf_nodes 12, min_samples_leaf 176, l2 2.7527). June, once: AP 0.565, 39 of 64 frauds in the 100 highest scores (logistic 0.539 / 36; regularized 0.556 / 36; default 0.477 / 32).
  • Threshold chosen on January to May, June cost: logistic 0.051 → 166 reviews, 41 caught, $10,285; regularized boosting 0.03 → 172, 41, $10,345; tuned boosting 0.04 → 165, 42, $9,900. Review nothing $24,000; the default 0.5 threshold costs $15,970 (logistic).
  • Night features (hour < 6, night × new device): logistic CV AP 0.532, June cost at a tuned threshold $10,120; boosting 0.53: small changes, within noise.
  • About 5.5 reviews a day in June at any of these thresholds (8,262 payments over 30 days).
The answer the data support

A review queue of about 165 to 190 June payments (about 6 a day), chosen by a model trained on January to May with a threshold tuned for the $10 / $375 costs, catches about 41 or 42 of 64 frauds and costs about $9,900 to $10,300 against $24,000 for reviewing nothing. Tuned boosting, regularized boosting and logistic regression are within a few hundred dollars of each other: about one fraud ($375), so June cannot rank them; choose the simplest one you can explain and monitor, and expect the fraud rate to keep drifting (1.00% in January, 0.77% in June).

Traps (and what they report)
  • Accuracy: reviewing nothing is 0.9923 accurate in June and catches no fraud.
  • A threshold tuned on June: the best June threshold for tuned boosting (0.03) shows $9,395, about $500 better than the honest $9,900: chosen by the data that grades it.
  • A stratified random split of all six months: AP 0.506 on 131 test frauds. The number is not dramatically inflated here, but the model is tested on months it trained around and the drift is hidden; the split does not match how the model is used.
  • Default boosting: CV AP 0.443: with 374 positives the defaults overfit; gentle settings or a search fix it.
  • Claiming a winner by a few hundred dollars: one fraud is $375 and June has 64.
  • transaction_id or the timestamp as a feature: ordering, not behavior (here it changes little: CV AP 0.524), and it breaks as soon as the model scores July.
What an honest result sounds like
  • "Trained on January to May, with the review threshold chosen on those months, the queue would have held 165 June payments (5.5 a day), caught 42 of 64 frauds and cost $9,900 against $24,000 for reviewing nothing. Logistic regression did almost as well ($10,285); the difference is about one fraud, so we cannot say which is better from June alone."
  • Follow-up questions: "Which months chose your threshold?" · "How many reviews a day does your queue need?" · "What would you watch in July to know the model still works?"
  • Weak submissions: accuracy; a random split; a June-tuned threshold; a winner declared on a $300 difference.

How These Notes Were Made

Every number above comes from the instructor's own run on the shipped course data (scikit-learn 1.9.1, pandas 3.0, 4 CPU threads, random_state=42 everywhere), using the scripts kitA_measure_churn.py, kitA_measure_demand.py and kitA_measure_fraud.py in the kit's generator folder. Learners will reach slightly different numbers with other reasonable choices (other candidates, another search budget, another threshold method): judge the split, the comparison and the honesty of the report, not agreement to the third decimal. The split sizes and the do-nothing numbers must match exactly.
