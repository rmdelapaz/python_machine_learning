Warm-up

Session 8 retrieval; the guide has what to say after each.

W1
Lesson 4.3: you raise a linear SVM's C from 0.01 to 100 on overlapping data. Street width and number of support vectors?
Narrower street, fewer support vectors (the lesson: 4.06 wide with 42 support vectors, then 0.75 with 7).
Why: C is the price of a violation.
W2
An RBF SVM with gamma=1000 scores 1.000 on training data and 0.650 on test data. What happened, and what would have warned you without the test set?
Each point's influence shrank to a tiny bubble: it memorized the training set. Cross-validation would have shown it, and so would the support-vector count (nearly every point).
Why: Today's boosting has the same trap with a new dial: the number of trees.
W3
A tree node holds 40 stayers and 10 churners. Gini impurity? Predict, then check.
import numpy as np
p = np.array([40, 10]) / 50
print(round(float(1 - np.sum(p ** 2)), 2))
0.32
Why: 1 - (0.64 + 0.04) = 0.32. A customer landing there gets churn probability 0.2.
W4
Why does an SVM need StandardScaler while a decision tree does not?
The SVM's kernel and margin are distances, so units matter. A tree only asks "above this threshold?", and rescaling moves the threshold, not the customers.
Why: Same for today's forests and boosted trees: no scaling.
W5
Homework check: a bootstrap sample of 4,500 rows drawn with replacement from 4,500. What share of the distinct customers does it contain?
About 63%. Lesson 5.2's first block shows 62.9% (2,830 customers); theory 63.2%.
Why: Collect the written predictions: most people guess 100% or 50%. Today opens with exactly this.

5.2 · Bagging and Random Forests: self-check

Q1
What does this print? (The share of distinct rows in a bootstrap sample of n rows from n.)
n = 1000
print(round(1 - (1 - 1 / n) ** n, 3))
0.632
Why: Each row is missed on one draw with probability 1 - 1/n, on all n draws with (1 - 1/n)^n, which approaches 1/e = 0.368. So about 63.2% of the distinct rows are in the sample and 36.8% are out-of-bag.
Q2
Which change makes the trees of a random forest LESS correlated with each other?
A) Raising n_estimators
B) Setting max_features=None
C) Lowering max_features
D) Raising min_samples_leaf to 500
Why: With fewer candidate features per split, trees are often denied the best feature and must find other splits, so their errors overlap less (the ρσ² term shrinks). Lab B measures it: correlation 0.357 at max_features=1 against 0.535 at 10.
A is wrong: More trees make the average more precise; each tree is just as alike as before.
B is wrong: That turns the forest back into bagging: the MOST correlated trees.
D is wrong: Huge leaves make every tree simple and similar.
Q3
For one training customer, which trees produce the out-of-bag prediction?
A) Only the trees whose bootstrap sample did not include that customer
B) All trees in the forest
C) Trees refitted on a held-out fold
D) The trees that predicted that customer correctly
Why: About 37% of the trees never saw a given customer; their average is an unseen-data prediction, made without holding anything out. That is why the OOB AUC (0.866) matches 5-fold CV (0.868) at a fifth of the cost.
B is wrong: Trees that trained on the customer memorized them: that is the 1.000 training AUC.
C is wrong: No refitting happens: one fit gives the OOB score.
D is wrong: That would be cheating with the answer.
Q4
What happens when this runs?
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
RandomForestClassifier(bootstrap=False, oob_score=True).fit([[0], [1], [2], [3]], [0, 0, 1, 1])
A) It fits, and oob_score_ is 1.0
B) It fits, and oob_score_ is missing
C) A warning that too few trees were used
D) ValueError: Out of bag estimation only available if bootstrap=True
Why: Without bootstrap sampling every tree sees every row, so no row is ever out of the bag. Keep bootstrap=True (the default) or use cross-validation.
A is wrong: It does not fit at all.
B is wrong: scikit-learn refuses the combination at fit time.
C is wrong: That warning is for too FEW trees with bootstrap on (Lesson 5.2, Common Mistake 3).
Q5
What does this print? (The variance of an average of n models with correlation 0.3.)
rho, var = 0.3, 1.0
print([round(rho * var + (1 - rho) * var / n, 3) for n in (1, 10, 100)])
[1.0, 0.37, 0.307]
Why: ρσ² + (1 - ρ)σ²/n: the second term shrinks with n, the first (0.3) stays. More trees cannot remove the error the trees share; less alike trees (smaller ρ) can.
Q6
A column of pure random noise gets an impurity importance (MDI) of 0.065 in a forest, but a permutation importance of 0.005 on the test set. Best conclusion?
A) The column carries real signal that permutation importance missed
B) MDI was inflated: a many-valued column wins chance splits on the training data
C) The column has a small causal effect on churn
D) The forest needs more trees
Why: MDI is computed from training-data splits and rewards columns with many distinct values. Shuffling the column on unseen data hardly hurts, so the splits did not generalize. Lab A shows the mirror image on fraud: the 0/1 column device_new is underrated by MDI (0.081) and ranks second by permutation (0.122).
A is wrong: Pure noise has no signal by construction.
C is wrong: Neither importance measures causes.
D is wrong: More trees average the noise splits; they do not stop them.

5.2 · Bagging and Random Forests: predict, then run

P1
Predict the output, then run it.
import numpy as np
votes = np.array([[1, 0, 1],      # tree 1's churn votes for three customers
                  [1, 1, 0],      # tree 2
                  [0, 0, 1],      # tree 3
                  [1, 0, 1]])     # tree 4
print(votes.mean(axis=0))
[0.75 0.25 0.75]
Why: Each column is one customer; the mean of the four 0/1 votes is the share of trees voting churn. A forest averages probabilities the same way.
P2
Predict the output, then run it.
rho, var = 0.5, 1.0
for n in [1, 10, 1000]:
    print(n, round(rho * var + (1 - rho) * var / n, 3))
1 1.0 10 0.55 1000 0.5
Why: With n = 1 the variance is σ² = 1. More models shrink only the second term; with correlation 0.5 the average never gets below 0.5.

5.3 · Gradient Boosting: self-check

Q7
What does this print? (Current predictions 20 and 20, truths 26 and 14, learning rate 0.1, a tree that fits the residuals perfectly.)
import numpy as np
pred = np.array([20.0, 20.0])
truth = np.array([26.0, 14.0])
print(pred + 0.1 * (truth - pred))
[20.6 19.4]
Why: Residuals +6 and -6, scaled by 0.1: each prediction moves a tenth of the way, to 20.6 and 19.4. The next tree aims at the remaining +5.4 and -5.4: a gentle putt.
Q8
Bagging and boosting both combine trees. Which statement is right?
A) Bagging averages independent deep trees and mainly reduces variance; boosting adds small trees in sequence, each fixing what is still wrong, and mainly reduces bias
B) Both grow deep trees on bootstrap samples; boosting just uses more of them
C) Boosting averages the trees; bagging adds them
D) Bagging needs early stopping; boosting never overfits
Why: A forest's trees never talk to each other; averaging cancels their quirks. Boosting's trees each aim at the remaining errors, so the model grows more detailed with every tree, which is why it needs early stopping and a forest does not.
B is wrong: Boosting's trees are small and trained on the residuals, not on bootstrap samples.
C is wrong: Backwards.
D is wrong: Backwards: more trees never make a forest overfit; boosting overfits if it runs unchecked.
Q9
You fit HistGradientBoostingClassifier() with all defaults on the 4,500 training customers. How many trees does it build?
A) It stops at the best number of trees on an internal validation set
B) 2,000
C) 100: early_stopping="auto" is off below 10,000 rows, so it builds max_iter=100
D) As many as there are features
Why: "auto" turns early stopping on only above 10,000 rows. On churn the validation loss is best at 27 trees, so the default 100 are already past it. On the 35,000 fraud payments (Lab B), "auto" is on and the default stops at 46.
A is wrong: Only above 10,000 rows, or with early_stopping=True.
B is wrong: max_iter defaults to 100.
D is wrong: The number of trees has nothing to do with the number of features.
Q10
What happens when this runs?
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
X = pd.DataFrame({"tenure": [3, 40, 5, 60], "store": ["Bend", "Boise", "Bend", "Online"]})
HistGradientBoostingClassifier().fit(X, [1, 0, 1, 0])
A) It fits, treating store as categorical
B) ValueError: could not convert string to float: 'Bend'
C) It fits, one-hot encoding store automatically
D) A warning, then store is dropped
Why: HistGradientBoosting treats a column as categorical only if its dtype is category. Text is treated as a number, and 'Bend' is not one. Fix: X[cols] = X[cols].astype("category") before splitting.
A is wrong: Only the category dtype is detected (categorical_features="from_dtype").
C is wrong: No library one-hot encodes for you.
D is wrong: Nothing is dropped silently.
Q11
You early-stop an XGBClassifier. What goes into eval_set?
A) The test set, so it stops where it will be graded
B) The training rows it is fitted on
C) Nothing: XGBoost holds out its own validation set
D) A validation set split off from the training rows
Why: Stopping on the test set makes the test score partly a training score (the lesson's peeking model reports 0.872; Lab B's 0.867 / AP 0.510). XGBoost and LightGBM do not hold out data for you; HistGradientBoosting does (validation_fraction).
A is wrong: That is peeking: the number of trees is chosen to suit the test set.
B is wrong: The training loss falls forever: it would never stop.
C is wrong: That is HistGradientBoosting's early_stopping=True, not XGBoost's.
Q12
You forecast weekly units sold (counts, never negative) with HistGradientBoostingRegressor and get 45 negative forecasts. What is the best first fix?
A) Clip the forecasts at zero and move on
B) Use loss="poisson", which predicts on a log scale
C) Scale the target with StandardScaler
D) Add more trees
Why: Poisson loss suits counts: every forecast is positive and effects become multiplicative (a promotion adds 30%, for socks and tents alike). In the lesson project it also lowered MAE from 8.16 to 8.05. Choosing the loss to match the target is often worth more than tuning.
A is wrong: Clipping hides the symptom; the model is still additive in the wrong way.
C is wrong: Scaling the target does not stop negative predictions.
D is wrong: More trees fit the same wrong loss harder.

5.3 · Gradient Boosting: predict, then run

P3
Predict the output, then run it.
import numpy as np
pred, truth = np.array([10.0]), np.array([14.0])
for step in range(3):
    pred = pred + 0.5 * (truth - pred)
    print(pred)
[12.] [13.] [13.5]
Why: Each step closes half of the remaining gap of 4: 10 → 12 → 13 → 13.5. A gentle learning rate never overshoots.
P4
Predict the output, then run it.
import numpy as np
rate = 0.2
start = np.log(rate / (1 - rate))
print(round(float(start), 3), round(float(1 / (1 + np.exp(-start))), 3))
-1.386 0.2
Why: log(0.2 / 0.8) = log(0.25) = -1.386; the sigmoid turns it back into 0.2. A boosted classifier starts there and adds trees on this scale.

Exit Ticket

  1. A forest and a boosted model are both made of trees. In two sentences: how are their trees different, and which kind of error does each mainly fix? Answer: A forest grows many deep, independent trees on bootstrap samples and averages them: it mainly reduces variance. Boosting grows small trees one after another, each fitted to what the model still gets wrong: it mainly reduces bias (and needs early stopping).
  2. Why is a forest's out-of-bag score free, and when should you not trust it? Answer: Each tree never saw about 37% of the training rows, so averaging only those trees gives an unseen-data prediction for every row from one fit. Do not trust it when preprocessing learned from the target, when rows are not independent (time or groups), or with too few trees.
  3. A colleague early-stops XGBoost with eval_set=[(X_test, y_test)] and reports AUC 0.867. What is wrong, and what do you tell them? Answer: The number of trees was chosen by watching the test set, so the test score is partly a training score. Early-stop on a validation set split off the training rows (or HistGradientBoosting's built-in early stopping) and touch the test set once.

Common Wrong Answers and What They Reveal

  • Q1 written as 0.5 or 1.0. Thinks a sample of n contains all or half the rows. Run the lesson's first block once more.
  • Q2 A (more trees). Confuses the two terms of the formula: n shrinks the second term only.
  • Q6 A. Trusts MDI. Show the noise column's two bars side by side.
  • Q8 B or D. The panel and the golfer have merged. Redraw both.
  • Q9 A. Assumes early stopping is always on; it is "auto" (above 10,000 rows).
  • Q11 A. The most dangerous one: they will report inflated numbers in their final project. Spend a minute on it at the start of Session 10.
  • Exit ticket 2: "OOB is always trustworthy". Revisit the target-encoding caveat before Lesson 6.1.
