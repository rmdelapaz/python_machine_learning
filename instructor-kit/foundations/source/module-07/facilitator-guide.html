Session 7: Nearest Neighbors and Naive Bayes

How to teach the seventh session of Machine Learning Foundations: k-nearest neighbors (distance as similarity, why scaling decides everything, the k dial, lookalikes as explanations, the curse of dimensionality) and Naive Bayes (Bayes' rule from counts, bag of words, smoothing, the variants, and a model that ranks well while its probabilities are too confident).

How to Use This Guide

  • The two lessons are opposites worth contrasting out loud: kNN has no weights and lives on distances (scaling is everything, more columns hurt); Naive Bayes counts and multiplies (no scaling, thrives on thousands of word columns, but double-counts duplicates).
  • Every code block below was run to produce the output printed under it; plotting lines are left out. Show the decision maps (4.1) and the bell-curve and calibration charts (4.2) from classification_knn.html and classification_naive_bayes.html.

The #1 risk of Session 7: kNN's lookalikes and Naive Bayes' probabilities taken at face value

Both models produce something that looks like an explanation or a confidence: "these 15 customers" and "99% sure". Learners leave trusting both. Protect two moments: the noise-columns step (4.1 Step 10: lookalikes are only as good as the distance) and the overconfidence check (4.2 Step 10: above 90% sure, 61% churned).

Questions and answers
  • W1. A log-target demand model gives holiday_week a coefficient of 0.405. In words? Answer: A holiday week sells about exp(0.405) − 1 ≈ 50% more, for every product. Percent effects come back today as likelihood ratios that multiply.
  • W2. A logistic regression flag has coefficient 0.69. What does this print, and what does it mean? (code on the slide) Answer: 1.99 The odds ratio: the flag about doubles the odds. Naive Bayes today also multiplies odds, one clue at a time.
  • W3. Degree 15 has training MSE 0.016 and test MSE 0.187 (noise floor 0.09). Which problem, and which dial helps? Answer: High variance (overfitting); a penalty such as Ridge's alpha, fewer features or more data. Today's dial is k: k = 1 is the degree-15 model of nearest neighbors.
  • W4. Temperature in °F and °C: Ridge gives [-0.607, -0.607], Lasso [-1.164, 0]. What does Lasso's zero mean? Answer: Only that the column was redundant given its twin, not that it has no effect. Naive Bayes has the opposite problem with twins: it counts both.
  • W5. Why must features be scaled before Ridge or Lasso? Answer: The penalty charges for weight size, and a feature's units decide how big its weight must be. For kNN scaling is even more basic: it defines what "similar" means.

Mental model to say

"Ask an experienced real-estate agent what a house will sell for. No formula: they pull up the comps, the five or six recently sold homes most like this one, and average their prices. That is kNN. Two questions decide whether the estimate is any good: what counts as similar (is one extra bedroom a bigger difference than ten years of age?) and how many comps (one is a gamble; fifty drag in houses that are not alike)."

Write the two dials on the board: distance (which columns, what scale) and k (variance at 1, bias at 500). "Nothing is learned in advance: fit stores the table, predict does the work. A lazy learner."

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • No scaler, or a scaler fitted outside the pipeline. Symptom: AUC near 0.77, neighbors that share only an order value. Coach: "Which column decides the distance? Print the shares."
  • k chosen by training AUC, or k = 5 left at the default. Symptoms: "k = 1 is perfect"; a ranking full of ties. Coach: "On which rows? How many distinct probabilities can k = 5 give?"
  • Lookalikes searched with an unscaled row, or too many columns (kneighbors). Symptom: lookalikes that look nothing like the customer. Coach: "Did the customer go through the same scaler as the stored rows?" (debug_knn.py has exactly this bug.)

Mental model to say

"A store manager has read every comment card for years. Before reading a new one, they have a hunch: three cards in four are happy. That is the prior. Then they read clue by clue. 'Returned' nudges them strongly toward unhappy; 'love' nudges them back. Each clue multiplies the odds. At the end the hunch has become a judgment: the posterior. The manager has one habit: they weigh each word on its own, as if words did not influence each other. That is the naive part. Often wrong, still surprisingly good."

Write Bayes' rule and, under it, the odds form: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio × likelihood ratio × ...

  2. Bayes' rule from counts (4m). "Prior, likelihood, posterior, for one word." 
    has_word = X_train.str.contains(r"\breturned\b", case=False)
is_neg = y_train == 0
p_neg = is_neg.mean()
p_word_given_neg = has_word[is_neg].mean()
p_word_given_pos = has_word[~is_neg].mean()
p_word = has_word.mean()
posterior = p_word_given_neg * p_neg / p_word
print(f"prior P(negative)          {p_neg:.3f}")
print(f"P(word | negative)         {p_word_given_neg:.4f}")
print(f"P(word | positive)         {p_word_given_pos:.4f}")
print(f"posterior P(negative|word) {posterior:.3f}")
print(f"check by direct counting   {is_neg[has_word].mean():.3f}")
    prior P(negative)          0.255
P(word | negative)         0.0406
P(word | positive)         0.0029
posterior P(negative|word) 0.828
check by direct counting   0.828
    The likelihood ratio (about 14) lifts 0.25 to 0.83. With one clue, Bayes' rule equals counting. With 20 words no combination repeats: that is why we need the naive assumption.

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • The wrong variant for the input. Raw text into the model (could not convert string to float), a scaler in front of MultinomialNB (Negative values), word counts into GaussianNB (Sparse data). Coach: "What does this variant expect: counts, flags or measurements?"
  • alpha = 0 or tiny. One word vetoes the rest. Coach: "What is P(word | class) for a word never seen in that class?"
  • Probabilities quoted as facts, or stop words removed by habit. Coach: "Above 90% sure: how many really churned?" and "Is 'not' a stop word?"

Exit ticket (3 questions, no notes, 4 minutes)
  1. Why does kNN need scaled features, and why does MultinomialNB refuse them? Answer: kNN's distance adds raw differences, so units decide similarity; MultinomialNB models counts, and scaling creates negative values.
  2. k = 1 gives training AUC 1.000 and test AUC 0.645. Name the problem and the fix. Answer: high variance (each customer is its own neighbor); choose a larger k by cross-validation.
  3. Prior P(negative) = 0.25; a word appears in 8% of negative and 2% of positive reviews. Posterior? Answer: odds 1/3 × 4 = 4/3, so P ≈ 0.57.
