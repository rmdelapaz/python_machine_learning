4.1

k-Nearest Neighbors

By the end you can
  • Explain how k-nearest neighbors classifies a row, and compute Euclidean and Manhattan distances by hand
  • Show why unscaled features wreck distances, and fix it with a scaler inside a pipeline
  • Choose k with cross-validation and connect small and large k to variance and bias
  • Recognize the curse of dimensionality and why irrelevant features hurt kNN more than a linear model
  • Use kneighbors to show the lookalike customers behind a prediction
Key ideas
  • The agent's comps. Predict from the k most similar past rows: a vote share for classification, an average for regression.
  • Distance defines similarity. Euclidean (p=2) or Manhattan (p=1); without scaling, the column with the biggest numbers decides.
  • Scaling decides everything: churn AUC 0.774 unscaled, 0.836 scaled, same k.
  • k is the dial. k = 1: variance (training AUC 1.000); huge k: bias. Choose k by cross-validation; prefer the middle of a flat plateau.
  • Lookalikes explain predictions, but they are other customers' data: summarize, do not print.
  • The curse of dimensionality. Irrelevant columns blur every distance; for kNN, feature selection is part of the model.
  • Lazy learner: fit stores, predict searches. Fast to train, slow to predict, ships with its training data.
Worked example: similar products

Find the three products most like a tent by price and launch year, with and without scaling. NearestNeighbors is kNN without the prediction: it only finds the closest rows.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

products = pd.read_csv("data/products_ml.csv")
cols = ["price", "launch_year"]
tent = products[products["category"] == "Tents"].iloc[[0]]
print(tent[["name", "price", "launch_year"]].to_string(index=False))
                            name  price  launch_year
Juniper Vane Drift 2-Person Tent 398.95         2024
for name, prep in [("unscaled", None), ("scaled", StandardScaler())]:
    X = products[cols] if prep is None else prep.fit_transform(products[cols])
    nn = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=4).fit(X)
    query = tent[cols] if prep is None else prep.transform(tent[cols])
    _, idx = nn.kneighbors(query)
    near = products.iloc[idx[0][1:]]
    print(name, list(zip(near["price"], near["launch_year"])))
unscaled [(409.95, 2021), (371.95, 2019), (426.95, 2025)]
scaled [(442.95, 2024), (426.95, 2025), (317.95, 2024)]

Unscaled, prices span hundreds of dollars and years only six, so "similar" means "similar price": the lookalikes come from 2019, 2021 and 2025. Scaled, a year counts as much as a standard deviation of price, and two of the three are 2024 products. Neither is "right": scaling is a modeling choice that defines similarity.

4.2

Naive Bayes

By the end you can
  • Compute a posterior probability by hand with Bayes' rule, starting from counts in a table
  • Explain the "naive" independence assumption and show what it costs when features repeat each other
  • Turn review text into word counts with CountVectorizer and classify it with MultinomialNB inside a pipeline
  • Explain what the smoothing parameter alpha prevents, and choose between the Multinomial, Bernoulli, Complement and Gaussian variants
  • Tell a model that ranks well apart from one whose probabilities you can take at face value
Key ideas
  • The store manager. A prior, clues that multiply the odds, a posterior.
  • Bayes' rule: P(class | clue) = P(clue | class) × P(class) / P(clue). Odds form: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio.
  • The naive assumption: clues are independent within a class, so their probabilities multiply. Often false; rankings still decent.
  • Bag of words: CountVectorizer makes one sparse column per word; word order is lost ("would not buy again").
  • Smoothing (alpha) adds a pretend count so no unseen word can veto the rest. Keep it above 0.
  • Variants: Multinomial or Complement for counts, Bernoulli for presence, Gaussian for measurements. Never scale count inputs.
  • Duplicated features are counted twice: removing them can help Naive Bayes.
  • Good ranking, overconfident probabilities: rank with it; recalibrate before quoting it.
Worked example: a two-word clue

Bayes' rule with a two-word clue. Is "waterproof" good news?

import pandas as pd
reviews = pd.read_csv("data/reviews.csv")
labeled = reviews[reviews["rating"] != 3]
neg = labeled["rating"] <= 2
for pattern in [r"\bwaterproof\b", r"\bnot waterproof\b"]:
    has = labeled["text"].str.contains(pattern, case=False)
    print(f"{pattern:22s} reviews {has.sum():4d}   P(negative | it) {neg[has].mean():.3f}")
print(f"prior P(negative) {neg.mean():.3f}")
\bwaterproof\b         reviews  145   P(negative | it) 0.310
\bnot waterproof\b     reviews   58   P(negative | it) 0.603
prior P(negative) 0.255

Before reading, a review is negative with probability 0.255. A review that mentions "waterproof" is negative 31% of the time; one that says "not waterproof" 60%. Most of the word's bad news comes from the pair, which a bag of single words cannot see: that is why the lesson project tries word pairs (ngram_range=(1, 2)).

Glossary

TermMeaning
k-nearest neighborsPredicts from the k most similar training rows
Euclidean / Manhattan distanceStraight-line distance / sum of absolute differences
lazy learnerA model whose fit only stores the data; the work happens at predict time
curse of dimensionalityIn many dimensions all points are about equally far apart
prior / posteriorBelief before / after seeing the evidence
likelihood ratioP(clue | class A) / P(clue | class B): how much a clue multiplies the odds
naive assumptionFeatures are independent within each class
bag of wordsOne column per word, counts per document, word order ignored
smoothing (alpha)A pretend count added to every word so no probability is zero
calibrationWhether predicted probabilities match real frequencies
