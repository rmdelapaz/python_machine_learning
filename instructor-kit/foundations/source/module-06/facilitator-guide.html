Questions and answers
  • W1. RFECV's curve is 0.8328 at 6 columns and 0.8333 at 11; the folds disagree by about 0.02. How many columns do you keep, and why? Answer: 6: the difference is far smaller than the noise between folds, and the smaller set is cheaper and sturdier (the tolerance rule). Checkpoint 1 asks for honest comparisons like this one.
  • W2. Review $10, missed fraud $375. What does this print? (code on the slide) Answer: [0 1 1 1] 10 / 375 ≈ 0.027, so every probability from 0.04 up is flagged. Valid only for calibrated probabilities.
  • W3. Where must SMOTE sit when you cross-validate, and what happens otherwise? Answer: Inside an imblearn pipeline, after the preprocessing; resampling first put synthetic near-copies into the validation folds and reported AP 0.91 instead of about 0.49. Same rule as feature selection: a step that learns from y lives inside the folds.
  • W4. Why must an L1 model be scaled before it selects columns? Answer: The penalty charges for the size of each weight, so without scaling it keeps columns in large units and drops columns in small units, whatever their usefulness. Lesson 3.2 today: every penalty needs scaled features.
  • W5. A class-weighted model's probabilities average 0.27 when the real fraud rate is 0.009. What does that change? Answer: Its ranking is about the same, but the probabilities are no longer calibrated: a cost formula applied to them flags almost everything. Tune the threshold with cross-validation instead. Today's 3.1 shows where those probabilities come from: the sigmoid of the log-odds.

Linear and Logistic Regression

60 min
Objectives (from the lesson page)
  • Explain what least squares does and fit LinearRegression, reading coef_ and intercept_ in the units of the data
  • Interpret a coefficient in a multiple regression as an effect "holding the other features constant", and spot when that reading breaks down
  • Use a log target with TransformedTargetRegressor when errors grow with the size of the prediction
  • Describe how logistic regression turns a weighted sum into a probability, and read its coefficients as log-odds and odds ratios
  • Fit a multiclass logistic regression and know which settings are current (C, l1_ratio) and which are gone
Timing in the room

Mental model to say

"Plot weekly snowshoe sales against temperature and lay a ruler across the cloud. Tilt it, slide it. Each dot sits some vertical distance from the ruler: its residual. Linear regression finds the one position where the squared residuals add up to the least. That is least squares."

Then the second picture, for yes or no: "Keep the weighted sum, but squash it through an S so it stays between 0 and 1. The sum before squashing is the log-odds; exp of a coefficient is an odds ratio." Write both formulas on the board and add the phrase every coefficient needs: holding the other features constant.

Live demo (you type, they follow)

New notebook lesson_3_1.

  1. A ruler on the Snow scatter (3m). "Snow gear, 2024 to learn from, 2025 to test: a forecast must not see the future." 
    import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

demand = pd.read_csv("data/weekly_demand.csv")
train, test = demand[demand["year"] == 2024], demand[demand["year"] == 2025]
snow_train = train[train["category"] == "Snow"]
snow_test = test[test["category"] == "Snow"]

line = LinearRegression()
line.fit(snow_train[["avg_high_temp_f"]], snow_train["units_sold"])
print(f"slope {line.coef_[0]:.3f} units per °F, intercept {line.intercept_:.2f}")
print(f"test R2 {line.score(snow_test[['avg_high_temp_f']], snow_test['units_sold']):.3f}")
print(f"test MAE {mean_absolute_error(snow_test['units_sold'], line.predict(snow_test[['avg_high_temp_f']])):.2f} units")
    slope -0.761 units per °F, intercept 60.18
test R2 0.492
test MAE 8.64 units
    Read it in units: about 0.76 fewer snow items per extra °F. The intercept (60 units at 0 °F) is just where the line crosses the axis. Show the lesson's scatter chart.
  2. No magic in fit (2m). "For one feature, the slope is covariance over variance, and the line passes through the averages." 
    x, y = snow_train["avg_high_temp_f"], snow_train["units_sold"]
slope = np.cov(x, y)[0, 1] / x.var()
intercept = y.mean() - slope * x.mean()
print(f"by hand: slope {slope:.3f}, intercept {intercept:.2f}")
    by hand: slope -0.761, intercept 60.18
  3. Where the line goes (1m). "Predict 30, 60 and 85 °F." 
    print(line.predict(pd.DataFrame({"avg_high_temp_f": [30, 60, 85]})).round(1))
    [37.4 14.5 -4.5]
    Minus 4.5 snowshoes in summer: a straight line has no idea sales stop at zero.

  5. The fan, and the fix (3m). "Show the residual plot on the lesson page: small sellers within a few units, big sellers off by dozens. Effects act in percent. Model the log."

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Reading a coefficient without its units or its company. "Promo is 70 times more important than temperature" (14.0 against 0.19), or "price barely matters" from the naive -0.53. Coach: "Per what? Across the feature's range? Holding which other columns constant?"
  • Probability and log-odds mixed up. "0.88 adds 0.88 to the churn probability." Coach: "Which scale is z on? What is exp(0.88)?" Have them compute one sigmoid by hand.
  • Coefficients read as causes, or twins trusted. "Make customers install the app and churn drops 30%"; a promo coefficient that looks absurd (Lab A). Coach: "Did anyone change the app flag and watch?" and "Is this column a copy of another?"

Mental model to say

"Three students prepare with last year's practice exam. One learns a single rule, 'demand is about 25', and is always a bit wrong: high bias. One memorizes every answer, typos included: perfect on the practice exam, lost on the real one, and a different practice exam would have made a different student: high variance. The third learns the patterns and ignores details too specific to trust. Regularization turns the memorizer into the third student: learn many things, but pay for every strong opinion."

Write expected error = bias² + variance + noise and draw the U. "Every model in this course has a dial like this: tree depth, k in kNN next week, boosting rounds. Today's dial is alpha."

  2. A curve where we know the truth (1m). "A sine wave plus noise with SD 0.3: even a perfect model has MSE about 0.09." 
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def true_curve(x):
    return np.sin(2 * np.pi * x)

def make_sample(n=25):
    x = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)
    return x.reshape(-1, 1), true_curve(x) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n)

X_small, y_small = make_sample()
X_new, y_new = make_sample(500)
print(X_small.shape, X_new.shape)
    (25, 1) (500, 1)
  3. Too simple, too flexible (3m). "Predict which degree has the best TEST error." 
    from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

def poly_model(degree, regressor=None):
    if regressor is None:
        regressor = LinearRegression()
    return make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree, include_bias=False), StandardScaler(), regressor)

for degree in [1, 4, 15]:
    m = poly_model(degree).fit(X_small, y_small)
    train_mse = mean_squared_error(y_small, m.predict(X_small))
    test_mse = mean_squared_error(y_new, m.predict(X_new))
    print(f"degree {degree:2d}   train MSE {train_mse:.3f}   test MSE {test_mse:.3f}")

degree  4   train MSE 0.025   test MSE 0.133
degree 15   train MSE 0.016   test MSE 0.187
    Degree 15 has the lowest training error, below the noise floor, and a worse test error than degree 4. Write the diagnostic: high/high = too simple; low/much higher = too flexible.
  4. Bias and variance, measured (3m). "Refit each degree on 100 fresh samples. Show the lesson's three-panel chart of 20 curves while it runs." 
    samples = [make_sample() for _ in range(100)]
x_eval = np.linspace(0.05, 0.95, 50).reshape(-1, 1)

def bias_variance(make_model):
    preds = np.array([make_model().fit(X_s, y_s).predict(x_eval) for X_s, y_s in samples])
    bias_sq = ((preds.mean(axis=0) - true_curve(x_eval.ravel())) ** 2).mean()
    variance = preds.var(axis=0).mean()
    return bias_sq, variance

rows = []
for degree in [1, 3, 4, 8, 15]:
    bias_sq, variance = bias_variance(lambda: poly_model(degree))
    rows.append({"degree": degree, "bias^2": bias_sq, "variance": variance, "expected MSE": bias_sq + variance + 0.09})
print(pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("degree").round(3))

    from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

def demand_model(regressor):
    return TransformedTargetRegressor(make_pipeline(features, regressor), func=np.log1p, inverse_func=np.expm1)

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Judging flexibility by training error. "Degree 15 is best, lowest error." Coach: "On which rows?" Point at the diagnostic on the board.
  • A penalty on unscaled features. Results change with units (dollars against thousands). Coach: "What does the penalty add up? Is a weight of 3.8 per $1,000 the same as 0.0038 per dollar?"
  • Choosing alpha with the test set, or C read the wrong way. Coach: "Which rows chose alpha?" and, for logistic regression, "C is the inverse: small C, strong penalty."

Exit ticket (3 questions, no notes, 4 minutes)
  1. A churn model gives discount_share (scaled) a coefficient of 0.67. Say what it means in one sentence, and one thing it does not mean. Answer: one standard deviation more discount reliance multiplies the odds of churning by exp(0.67) ≈ 1.96, other features held constant; it does not mean discounts cause churn.
  2. Training error tiny, test error large: name the problem and two fixes. Answer: high variance; more penalty (alpha chosen by CV), fewer columns or more data.
  3. Why must features be scaled before Ridge or Lasso? Answer: the penalty charges for weight size, and units decide how large a weight must be.
