2.4

Feature Selection

By the end you can
  • Explain what feature selection buys you (simpler, cheaper, sturdier models) and what it usually does not (big accuracy gains)
  • Find redundant features with correlations, exact relationships and cross-tabulations
  • Use filter (SelectKBest), wrapper (RFECV, SequentialFeatureSelector) and embedded (L1, SelectFromModel) methods, and say what each can miss
  • Keep selection inside a Pipeline so cross-validation stays honest, and show what happens when it is not
  • Choose the smallest feature set whose cross-validated score is within a tolerance of the best
Key ideas
  • The ultralight backpack. Redundant items (two stoves) and irrelevant ones (the unused chair) go home; the goal is the lightest pack that still finishes the trail: the smallest feature set that keeps the score.
  • Selection buys simplicity, not accuracy. Fewer inputs to collect, explain and monitor; on a good set of columns the score barely moves.
  • Ask how a column was made. Averages, ratios, tiers and lookups computed from other columns are candidates to drop. Correlation misses relationships between three columns (spend = orders × average order value).
  • Filters (SelectKBest with f_classif or mutual_info_classif) score each column alone: fast, but blind to the model and to teamwork. F sees differences in means; mutual information sees any dependence but is noisy.
  • Wrappers (RFECV, SequentialFeatureSelector) try subsets with the real model: they find columns that matter together, at the cost of many fits.
  • Embedded: an L1 penalty (l1_ratio=1, solver="liblinear") zeros weak weights; smaller C means more zeros. Always scale first.
  • Selection that sees the labels is learning. Keep it inside the pipeline, or cross-validation lies: about 80% on pure noise.
  • The tolerance rule: read the curve, not the winner; take the smallest set within a small tolerance of the best, and test once. "Dropped" means "adds nothing given the others", not "irrelevant".

import pandas as pd
demand = pd.read_csv("data/weekly_demand.csv")
print("floor areas per store  :", demand.groupby("store")["store_sqft"].nunique().to_dict())
print("list prices per product:", demand.groupby("product_id")["list_price"].nunique().max())

Each store has exactly one floor area, and each product one list price, so once a model knows the store and the product, store_sqft and list_price add nothing. And price is list_price × (1 − discount_pct / 100) to the cent. Related is not the same as redundant, though:

2.5

Imbalanced Classes

By the end you can
  • Explain why accuracy fails on rare classes and judge a model with recall, precision and average precision against the right baseline
  • Apply class_weight="balanced" and the imbalanced-learn samplers (random under- and oversampling, SMOTE) inside a leak-proof pipeline
  • Show that weights and resampling mostly move the decision threshold, and explain what they do to predicted probabilities
  • Choose a threshold from the cost of each kind of mistake, by formula and with TunedThresholdClassifierCV
  • Check who carries the burden of false alarms before a model goes live
Key ideas
  • The smoke detector. Never beeping is right 364 days a year and worthless; beeping at toast catches every fire and gets unplugged. Where to sit between depends on what each mistake costs.
  • The accuracy paradox. At 0.876% fraud, "all legitimate" is 99.1% accurate and catches nothing. Never compare imbalanced models by accuracy (or by cross_val_score without scoring=).
  • RANK, then DECIDE. Judge the ranking with average precision against the base rate (about 0.009 here, not 0.5) and ROC AUC; judge decisions with recall, precision and cost.
  • Class weights and samplers mostly move the cut. On fraud, class_weight="balanced", under- and oversampling and SMOTE all gave about 1,900 flags, recall near 0.7 and no better ranking, and inflated the probabilities (mean 0.27 against a real 0.009).
  • Samplers go inside an imblearn pipeline. A scikit-learn pipeline refuses them; resampling before CV faked an AP of 0.91 (honest: 0.49).
  • The threshold formula: flag when p × (loss from a miss) > (cost of a review), so p > 10 / 375 ≈ 0.027. It needs calibrated probabilities.
  • TunedThresholdClassifierCV picks a threshold for any scorer with cross-validation on the training rows; FixedThresholdClassifier applies one you choose.
  • False alarms land on real customers. Check whether one group carries more than its share.
Worked example: a threshold from a capacity

Lesson 2.5's "Choosing a Different Target" box: the fraud team can review only about 20 payments a day. Choose the cut that would have filled that queue in January to May, then use it on June, without looking at June first.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder, StandardScaler

tx = pd.read_csv("data/transactions.csv")
cols = ["account_age_days", "amount", "hour", "items", "device_new", "billing_shipping_mismatch",
        "country_mismatch", "card_attempts", "velocity_24h", "category"]
is_june = tx["timestamp"] >= "2025-06-01"
train, june = tx[~is_june], tx[is_june]
prep = ColumnTransformer([("num", StandardScaler(), cols[:-1]),
                          ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), ["category"])])
model = make_pipeline(prep, LogisticRegression()).fit(train[cols], train["is_fraud"])
days_train = train["timestamp"].str[:10].nunique()
print(len(train), "training payments over", days_train, "days")
41738 training payments over 151 days
# the score that would have filled a 20-a-day queue in January to May
p_train = model.predict_proba(train[cols])[:, 1]
share = 20 * days_train / len(train)
threshold = np.quantile(p_train, 1 - share)
print(f"queue share {share:.2%}, threshold {threshold:.3f}")

flags = model.predict_proba(june[cols])[:, 1] >= threshold
caught = int(june.loc[flags, "is_fraud"].sum())
missed = int(june["is_fraud"].sum()) - caught
print(f"June: {flags.sum()} flagged ({flags.sum() / 30:.1f} a day), {caught} of {june['is_fraud'].sum()} frauds caught")
print(f"cost at $10 a review and $375 a missed fraud: ${10 * flags.sum() + 375 * missed:,}")
queue share 7.24%, threshold 0.017
June: 596 flagged (19.9 a day), 47 of 64 frauds caught
cost at $10 a review and $375 a missed fraud: $12,335

The capacity threshold (0.017) is lower than the cost-tuned one from the lesson project (0.051), so June gets about 20 flags a day instead of 5.5. It catches 47 frauds instead of 41, but costs $12,335 against the tuned model's $10,285. Capacity is a limit, not a target: use it to rule thresholds out, and let cost choose among the rest.

Glossary

TermMeaning
redundant featureA column the others already determine (spend = orders × average order value)
filter methodScores each column against the target on its own (SelectKBest, VarianceThreshold)
wrapper methodTries subsets of columns with the real model (RFECV, SequentialFeatureSelector)
embedded methodSelects while training, such as an L1 penalty that sets weights to exactly zero
L1 penaltyA charge on the total size of the weights; l1_ratio=1 in LogisticRegression, smaller C = more zeros
tolerance ruleKeep the smallest set whose CV score is within a small tolerance of the best
imbalanced classesOne class (the positive, minority class) is much rarer than the other
average precision (AP)Area under the precision-recall curve; its baseline is the positive rate
class weightA multiplier on each class's training mistakes; "balanced" = n / (2 × class count)
resamplingChanging the training rows: undersampling, oversampling, SMOTE (synthetic minority rows)
calibratedPredicted probabilities that match real frequencies (a 0.2 means about one in five)
decision thresholdThe probability above which the model says "positive"; 0.5 by default
