Session 3: Metrics and Preprocessing

How to teach Session 3 of Machine Learning Foundations. The first half turns "84.8% accurate" into the numbers a business can act on: the confusion matrix, precision and recall, a threshold chosen from costs on training data only, ranking metrics, and honest regression errors. The second half brings the rest of the churn table into the model: gaps filled (and remembered), units scaled, categories encoded, all in one ColumnTransformer learned from the training rows. Learners leave knowing that the same model can earn $2,280 or $3,555 depending only on its threshold, and that preparing all 16 columns lifts this linear model's ROC AUC only from 0.823 to 0.828: an honest result they could finally measure.

The #1 risk of Session 3: the threshold gets chosen on the test set

Lesson 1.4 gives learners a powerful new dial, and the natural thing to do with a dial is to turn it until the test score looks best. Every time someone in the room picks a threshold from a test-set table, say it out loud: "That is tuning on the test set." The demo shows the honest routes (out-of-fold probabilities, TunedThresholdClassifierCV), the lab practices one, and the project is built on it. The same risk returns after the break in a quieter form: fit_transform on the test rows. It raises no error. The checks in Step 4 of the 2.1 project (training columns centered exactly, test columns only close to center) and the debug_preprocess.py handout are there to make it visible. Second risk: the pace. Two dense lessons; protect the order below and cut from the bottom of the list.

Warm-up questions and answers
  1. W1 (Lesson 1.2). Why does every churn split in this course pass stratify=y? Answer: It keeps the churn share (16.5%) the same in the training and test parts. Without it the class balance wobbles from split to split, and with it the baseline and every score wobble too.
  2. W2 (Lesson 1.2). In the honest-evaluation project, a depth-2 tree on the single column has_reason scored 0.942 on validation against a baseline of 0.835. What do you do, and what do you check first? Answer: Treat it as a leak suspect: far too good for one column. Check when and how it is recorded (it is filled only when an account is closed, after the outcome), then drop it. A high score alone is not proof; the recording process is.
  3. W3 (Lesson 1.3). Why cross-validate make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression()) instead of scaling X_train once and cross-validating the model on the scaled table? Answer: The pipeline refits the scaler on the training folds of every round. Scaling first lets each validation fold shape the mean and standard deviation: a small, hidden leak, and a big one when the preprocessing learns more (today's imputers and encoders).
  4. W4 (Lesson 1.3). What does cross_val_predict(model, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, method="predict_proba")[:, 1] give you, and why is it honest? Answer: One churn probability per training customer, each from the fold model that did NOT train on that customer. Today's project chooses its decision threshold from exactly these out-of-fold probabilities, so the test set stays sealed.
  5. W5 (Homework check (1.3, Before the Next Lesson)). You reran the bake-off with scoring=["accuracy", "roc_auc", "f1"]. What did the baseline score on accuracy and on F1, and why so different? Answer: Accuracy 0.835, ROC AUC 0.5, F1 0.0. The baseline never predicts churn, so it has no true positives: recall is 0 and precision is 0/0 (scikit-learn reports 0 with a warning), so F1 is 0. Accuracy rewards the 83.5% it gets right by saying "stays". That gap is today's first topic.

The W5 numbers, from a real run of the bake-off's baseline with three scorers (DummyClassifier(strategy="prior"), 5 stratified folds on the 4,500 training customers):

Mental model to say

Say: "A smoke alarm that never goes off is right on almost every day of the year, because almost every day has no fire. Nobody would call it a good alarm. What you want to know is two things. When there is a fire, does it go off? That is recall. When it goes off, is there really a fire? That is precision. And how sensitive to set it, the threshold, depends on what a false alarm costs compared with a missed fire."

Then point back at the warm-up's three numbers: "The baseline is the alarm that never goes off: 83.5% accurate, F1 zero." And the business frame for the whole lesson, written under the 2×2: a wasted call costs a little, a missed churner costs a lot. "Accuracy treats both mistakes the same. Pinecrest does not."

Live demo (you type, they follow)

  1. Same split, two models, one question (3m). "Lesson 1.1 left us with an argument: 84.8% accurate, baseline 83.5%. Is the model any good? Today we stop arguing and count." The logistic regression gets its scaler inside a pipeline, as in Lesson 1.3. 
    import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

            "returns_12m", "return_rate", "support_tickets_12m", "discount_share", "app_user"]
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    customers[features], customers["churned"], test_size=0.25, stratify=customers["churned"], random_state=42

  8. Put money on the four cells (4m). Write on the board: offer $15 to anyone contacted; $60 for each real churner reached; nothing for customers left alone. "Profit = 60 × TP − 15 × (TP + FP)." Ask for predictions: contact everyone? The default threshold? 
    OFFER_COST = 15     # dollars per customer contacted
CHURNER_VALUE = 60  # expected dollars per real churner contacted


def campaign_profit(y_true, flagged):
    """Expected profit of contacting every flagged customer."""
    y_true, flagged = np.asarray(y_true), np.asarray(flagged)
    tp = ((flagged == 1) & (y_true == 1)).sum()
    return CHURNER_VALUE * tp - OFFER_COST * flagged.sum()


print("contact nobody   ", campaign_profit(y_test, np.zeros(len(y_test), dtype=int)))
print("contact everyone ", campaign_profit(y_test, np.ones(len(y_test), dtype=int)))
print("threshold 0.5    ", campaign_profit(y_test, y_pred))
    contact nobody    0
contact everyone  -7620
threshold 0.5     2280
    Contacting everyone LOSES $7,620. Then: "If the probabilities were perfect, contact everyone above 15 / 60 = 0.25, the break-even. They are not perfect, so we measure."
  9. Choose the threshold honestly (5m). "TunedThresholdClassifierCV cross-validates on the training rows only, tries 100 thresholds per fold, keeps the best average profit. make_scorer turns our profit function into a scorer." Warn: this cell takes a few seconds. 
    from sklearn.metrics import make_scorer
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, TunedThresholdClassifierCV

profit_scorer = make_scorer(campaign_profit)
cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)

tuned = TunedThresholdClassifierCV(logreg, scoring=profit_scorer, cv=cv).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(f"chosen threshold {tuned.best_threshold_:.3f}")
print(f"mean profit per validation fold (900 customers) {tuned.best_score_:,.0f}")
print(f"test-set profit {campaign_profit(y_test, tuned.predict(X_test)):,}")
    chosen threshold 0.245
mean profit per validation fold (900 customers) 2,603
test-set profit 3,555
    0.245, next to the break-even 0.25: the logistic regression's probabilities are reasonably calibrated. Same model, same probabilities, only the decision rule changed: $3,555 instead of $2,280. The fold profit (900 customers) and the test profit (1,500) are not comparable.

  11. Grade the demand forecast against two baselines (3m). "Train on 2024, forecast 2025: a time split from Lesson 1.2. Two baselines: the overall mean, and each product-and-store pair's 2024 average." 
    from sklearn.dummy import DummyRegressor
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, root_mean_squared_error, r2_score

                   "avg_high_temp_f", "store_sqft", "week_of_year"]

test_d = demand[demand["year"] == 2025]

predictions = {
    "overall mean": DummyRegressor().fit(train_d[demand_features], train_d["units_sold"]).predict(test_d[demand_features]),
    "product-store mean": test_d.join(pair_mean, on=["product_id", "store"])["pair_mean"].to_numpy(),
}
hgb = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=42).fit(train_d[demand_features], train_d["units_sold"])
predictions["gradient boosting"] = hgb.predict(test_d[demand_features])

y_d = test_d["units_sold"]
for name, pred in predictions.items():
    print(f"{name:19s} MAE {mean_absolute_error(y_d, pred):6.2f}   RMSE {root_mean_squared_error(y_d, pred):6.2f}   "
          f"R2 {r2_score(y_d, pred):6.3f}")
    overall mean        MAE  17.13   RMSE  26.21   R2 -0.006

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab:

  • Recall or precision: "of the customers we called, how many were really leaving?" Answer: precision.
  • You lower the threshold. Which goes up, which goes down, and what stays the same? Answer: recall up, precision down (usually); ROC AUC does not change, because it does not depend on any threshold.
  • Why is 0.165 the baseline for average precision on churn, not 0.5? Answer: a random ranking has precision equal to the churn rate at every recall.
  • Offer $15, churner reached $60: what is the break-even probability, and why did the tuner choose 0.245 and not exactly 0.25? Answer: 0.25; the tuner measured profit on out-of-fold data, and real probabilities are only roughly calibrated.

Mental model to say

Preprocessing is mise en place for a model. Each kind of ingredient gets its own treatment: you peel carrots, you do not peel eggs. Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab:

  • What exactly does a fitted SimpleImputer remember, and where? Answer: one fill value per column, in statistics_, learned from the training rows.
  • Median or mean for email opens, and why? Answer: median: the count is right-skewed (a few very active readers pull the mean up to 7.28 against a median of 6).
  • A RobustScaler and a StandardScaler: which one does a single $250,000 data-entry error distort, and why? Answer: StandardScaler, because one extreme value inflates the standard deviation; RobustScaler uses the median and the interquartile range.
  • Why one-hot for home_store but OrdinalEncoder for loyalty_tier? Answer: stores have no order; tiers do (Basic < Silver < Gold), and the order must be spelled out.
  • A colleague's table also contains cancellation_reason. What does your ColumnTransformer do with it? Answer: drops it: it is in no branch and the default is remainder="drop".

Closing

Exit ticket (3 questions, no notes, 4 minutes)

Put the three questions on the slide. Learners answer in the workbook's Exit Ticket box or on a sticky note and leave it on the way out. Sort the notes into "got it" and "not yet" before Session 4: the pile for question 2 tells you whether to open Session 4's warm-up with the threshold again; the pile for question 3 tells you how much of 2.2's opening to spend on fit versus transform.

  1. A model's confusion matrix: TN 900, FP 100, FN 30, TP 70. Compute its precision and recall, and say each in one business sentence. Answer: precision 0.412 recall 0.700. Of the customers we flag, about four in ten really churn; we catch seven in ten of the churners.
  2. Why choose a decision threshold from out-of-fold training probabilities instead of the test set? Answer: the test set must stay unseen to give an honest final number; out-of-fold probabilities come from models that never saw each customer, so the choice is honest and the test set is used once.
  3. Next month's customer comes from the new Reno store and did not give an age. What does each part of your fitted preprocessor do with them? Answer: the one-hot branch encodes Reno as all zeros (handle_unknown="ignore") and the missing age as the age_group_nan column; the scalers apply the training means and standard deviations; nothing is refitted.
