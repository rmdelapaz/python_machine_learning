Warm-up

Session 7 retrieval. The answers below are also in the guide, with what to say next.

W1
Lesson 4.1: a kNN churn model scores a perfect 1.000 on its training data with k=1. Why, and what do you do about it?
Each training customer is its own nearest neighbor, so k=1 is right by construction (high variance). Choose a larger k by cross-validation; the 4.1 project chose k=51.
Why: Ask for the word: overfitting. Today's two models have their own versions: gamma for SVMs, depth for trees.
W2
Two unscaled features: spend differs by $300 and days since last order by 120. What share of the squared distance comes from spend? Predict, then check.
import numpy as np
spend, days = 300, 120
print(round(spend ** 2 / (spend ** 2 + days ** 2), 2))
0.86 (86%)
Why: 300² = 90,000 against 120² = 14,400: spend takes 86% of the distance purely because of its units. The same reason SVMs need a scaler today.
W3
Lesson 4.2: a word appears in 6% of negative reviews and 0.5% of positive ones; 25% of reviews are negative. After seeing the word, about how likely is the review to be negative?
About 0.8: the odds go from 1:3 to (1 × 6) : (3 × 0.5) = 6 : 1.5 = 4 : 1.
Why: Prior odds times the likelihood ratio (12). Anyone stuck: draw the 1,000-review table.
W4
Why does MultinomialNB need alpha above 0?
Without smoothing a word never seen in one class gives that class a probability of exactly 0, which vetoes all the other evidence.
Why: Then bridge: today's SVM on reviews (Lab A) needs no smoothing, but it needs scaling, in its text form (TF-IDF).
W5
Your Naive Bayes model ranks customers well (AUC 0.81) but its probabilities pile up near 0 and 1. Can you quote them to the care team?
Rank with it, but recalibrate (CalibratedClassifierCV) before quoting probabilities.
Why: Today's SVM gives no probabilities at all, and the same tool fixes it.

4.3 · Support Vector Machines: self-check

Q1
On overlapping classes you lower a linear SVM's C from 1 to 0.01. What do you expect?
A) A narrower street and fewer support vectors
B) A wider street held up by many more support vectors
C) No change: C only matters for the RBF kernel
D) A perfect training score, because violations are now free
Why: C is the price of a margin violation. Cheap violations let the SVM widen the street and accept many points inside it, and every point on or inside the margin is a support vector. The lesson's blobs: 11 support vectors and width 1.34 at C=1, 42 and 4.06 at C=0.01.
A is wrong: That is what RAISING C does (C=100: 7 support vectors, width 0.75).
C is wrong: C prices violations for every kernel, linear included.
D is wrong: Free violations make the model simpler, not a better fit to the training points.
Q2
What does this print?
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC
pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC())
print([key for key in pipe.get_params() if key.endswith("gamma")])
['svc__gamma']
Why: make_pipeline names each step after its class in lowercase, and a nested parameter is the step name, two underscores, then the parameter. This is the key a grid search needs; gamma alone raises Invalid parameter.
Q3
Why does an RBF SVC on the churn table need a StandardScaler in front of it?
A) The SVM cannot read integer columns
B) Scaling makes the SVM train much faster, nothing else changes
C) Without scaling the model cannot output probabilities
D) The kernel and the margin are built on distances, and unscaled spend_12m and days_since_last_order would drown out the 0-to-1 columns
Why: Distances add up squared differences, so columns measured in hundreds dominate columns measured in fractions. In the lesson, scaling lifted the AUC from 0.767 to 0.814. Put the scaler inside the pipeline so cross-validation stays leak-free.
A is wrong: Integers are numbers; the SVM reads them fine.
B is wrong: Speed may change, but the reason is the distance: the AUC changes.
C is wrong: An SVC never outputs probabilities, scaled or not; that is what calibration is for.
Q4
What happens when this runs?
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC
pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC()).fit([[0], [1], [5], [6]], [0, 0, 1, 1])
print(pipe.predict_proba([[3]]))
A) It prints [[0.5 0.5]]: the point is halfway
B) AttributeError: This 'Pipeline' has no attribute 'predict_proba'
C) It prints [1]
D) NotFittedError: the pipeline was not fitted
Why: An SVM scores signed distances, not probabilities, so predict_proba does not exist; the pipeline passes the question to its last step, hence "Pipeline" in the message. Rank with decision_function, or wrap the SVM in CalibratedClassifierCV(SVC(), ensemble=False) for probabilities.
A is wrong: No probabilities are computed at all.
C is wrong: That would be predict.
D is wrong: .fit(...) is chained on the same line, so the pipeline is fitted.
Q5
On churn (16.5% churners) you add class_weight="balanced" to an RBF SVM. In the lesson, what happened?
A) Recall of churners jumped (0.258 to 0.774) while accuracy fell (0.859 to 0.792)
B) Nothing: class weights only affect logistic regression
C) Accuracy rose and recall fell
D) The model started predicting churn for every customer
Why: A mistake on a churner now costs about five times as much, so the boundary moves toward the stayers: many more churners are caught, more stayers are flagged, and the ranking (AUC 0.814 to 0.850) improves too.
B is wrong: SVC, logistic regression and trees all accept class_weight.
C is wrong: The opposite: the boundary moves to catch more churners.
D is wrong: It flagged many more customers, not all of them; accuracy 0.792 is far above what flagging everyone (0.165) would score.
Q6
What does this print? (RBF similarity of two points at squared distance 4, for two values of gamma.)
import numpy as np
print(round(float(np.exp(-0.1 * 4)), 3), round(float(np.exp(-10 * 4)), 3))
0.67 0.0
Why: Similarity is exp(-gamma × squared distance). With a small gamma the two points are still fairly similar (0.67): each support vector reaches far. With gamma=10 the similarity is essentially 0: each point's influence is a tiny bubble, the recipe for memorizing noise.

4.3 · Support Vector Machines: predict, then run

P1
Predict the output, then run it.
from sklearn.svm import SVC
m = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1).fit([[0], [1], [3], [4]], [0, 0, 1, 1])
print(m.support_vectors_.ravel(), m.predict([[1.8], [2.2]]))
[1. 3.] [0 1]
Why: The street's center is at 2 and its curbs at 1 and 3, so the support vectors are exactly the points 1 and 3. 1.8 is left of the center (class 0), 2.2 right of it (class 1).
P2
Predict the output, then run it.
import numpy as np
distance_squared = 4
for gamma in [0.1, 1, 10]:
    print(gamma, round(float(np.exp(-gamma * distance_squared)), 4))
0.1 0.6703 1 0.0183 10 0.0
Why: exp(-0.4) = 0.6703, exp(-4) = 0.0183, exp(-40) is essentially 0: the larger gamma, the faster similarity fades with distance.

5.1 · Decision Trees: self-check

Q7
What does this print? (A node with 30 stayers and 10 churners.)
import numpy as np
counts = np.array([30, 10])
p = counts / counts.sum()
print(round(float(1 - np.sum(p ** 2)), 3))
0.375
Why: Shares 0.75 and 0.25: Gini = 1 - (0.5625 + 0.0625) = 0.375. Between a pure node (0) and a 50/50 node (0.5).
Q8
Where does a decision tree's predicted churn probability for a new customer come from?
A) A weighted sum of the features, squashed into 0 to 1
B) The distance from the new customer to the boundary
C) The share of churners among the training customers in the leaf the new customer lands in
D) The average of many trees' votes
Why: Predicting means walking down the signposts to a campsite and reading its sign. That is why a depth-2 tree has only four possible probabilities, and an unlimited tree (pure leaves) gives only 0s and 1s.
A is wrong: That is logistic regression.
B is wrong: That is an SVM's decision_function.
D is wrong: That is a random forest (Lesson 5.2).
Q9
What does this print?
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
t = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1, random_state=42).fit([[1], [2], [8], [15]], [0, 0, 1, 1])
print(t.tree_.threshold[0])
A) 2.0
B) 5.0
C) 8.0
D) 6.5
Why: A tree tests thresholds halfway between neighboring sorted values. The only split that separates the classes falls between 2 and 8, at their midpoint 5.0.
A is wrong: The threshold is not one of the training values.
C is wrong: Same: the split sits between values, not on one.
D is wrong: 6.5 is the mean of the four values; thresholds are midpoints between neighboring values, not averages of the column.
Q10
An unlimited churn tree scores 1.000 on the training set and 0.822 on the test set, below the 0.835 baseline. What is the best next move?
A) Report 1.000: the tree learned the data perfectly
B) Scale the features, then refit
C) Switch the criterion from gini to entropy
D) Choose a size control such as min_samples_leaf by cross-validation on the training set
Why: A perfect training score is a warning: the tree memorized individual customers. In the lesson, min_samples_leaf=50 chosen by CV lifted the CV AUC from 0.663 to 0.848, with 58 leaves instead of 523.
A is wrong: A training score says nothing about new customers.
B is wrong: Scaling cannot change a tree's predictions: thresholds just change units.
C is wrong: The impurity measure matters far less than the size controls.
Q11
In the pruned churn tree, spend_12m gets an importance of 0.012, yet churn falls from 24% to 8% across spend quartiles. What is the right sentence?
A) The tree did not need spend: other features it split on first carried the same information
B) Spend has no relationship with churn
C) Raising a customer's spend would not change their churn risk
D) The importance is wrong because the features were not scaled
Why: Impurity importance describes how THIS tree used each column. Spend overlaps with tenure, recency and orders, so whichever the tree picked first took the credit. Say "the model did not need it", never "it does not matter".
B is wrong: The quartile table shows a clear relationship.
C is wrong: Importance is not a causal statement either way.
D is wrong: Trees do not need scaling, and importances do not depend on it.
Q12
What happens when this runs?
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=0).fit([[1], [2]], [0, 1])
A) A tree with no questions that predicts the majority class
B) A stump: one question
C) InvalidParameterError: max_depth must be at least 1 (or None)
D) An unlimited tree, because 0 means no limit
Why: scikit-learn checks hyperparameters when you call fit. The smallest legal tree is a stump (max_depth=1); None means no limit; a no-questions model is DummyClassifier.
A is wrong: That is DummyClassifier; the tree refuses depth 0.
B is wrong: That would be max_depth=1.
D is wrong: No limit is None, not 0.

5.1 · Decision Trees: predict, then run

P3
Predict the output, then run it.
import numpy as np
def gini(counts):
    p = np.asarray(counts) / np.sum(counts)
    return round(float(1 - np.sum(p ** 2)), 3)
print(gini([50, 50]), gini([90, 10]), gini([100, 0]))
0.5 0.18 0.0
Why: A 50/50 node is the most mixed (0.5); 90/10 gives 1 - (0.81 + 0.01) = 0.18; a pure node 0.
P4
Predict the output, then run it.
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
X, y = [[1], [2], [3], [10], [11], [12]], [0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1]
t = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1, random_state=42).fit(X, y)
print(t.tree_.threshold[0], t.predict_proba([[2.4], [11]]).round(2).tolist())
2.5 [[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]]
Why: The best split is between 2 and 3 (midpoint 2.5), which separates the classes perfectly, so both leaves are pure: 2.4 goes left (class 0 with probability 1.0), 11 right.

Exit Ticket

  1. Your SVM scores 0.996 on the training customers and AUC 0.699 on the test set, with 4,491 of 4,500 customers as support vectors. Which dial is set wrong, and how do you choose it properly? Answer: gamma is far too large (each customer's influence is a tiny bubble, so the model memorized the training set; nearly every point is a support vector). Choose C and gamma together with cross-validation on the training set (keys svc__C, svc__gamma), never with the training score.
  2. A node holds 40 stayers and 10 churners. What is its Gini impurity, and what churn probability does a tree give a customer who lands there? Answer: Gini = 1 - (0.8² + 0.2²) = 0.32. The probability is the leaf's churn share: 10 / 50 = 0.2.
  3. The care team wants rules they can pin to the wall AND the best possible top-50 list. What do you give them from today's two models, and why not one model for both? Answer: A small tree (about 12 leaves, size chosen by CV) as the rule book: readable, but its probabilities come in a few big ties. For the list, a model that scores every customer individually: today the tuned SVM (34 of 50; AUC 0.848), and next session a forest (41 of 50). Readability and ranking pull in different directions.

Common Wrong Answers and What They Reveal

  • Q1 A (narrower street). Has C backwards. Use the price picture: cheap violations, wide street.
  • Q3 B ("scaling is for speed"). Has not connected kernels with distance. Replay demo Step 7: 0.767 to 0.814.
  • Q5 C (accuracy up, recall down). Thinks class weights "improve" the model in every metric. Redraw the boundary moving toward the stayers.
