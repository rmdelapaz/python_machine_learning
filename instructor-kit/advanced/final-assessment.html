Answer Notes · Problem A: Demand forecasting

A sound project: steps
  • Plan the decision as an order placed ahead of the week: features are what is known then (store, product, category, calendar, list price, planned discount and promo, holiday week). Actual weekly temperature is not.
  • Keep the template split (2024 train with 5,088 rows, 2025 test with 4,896). Inside 2024, rolling-origin folds by week; note that they validate on unseen months and are pessimistic (Session 12 harness: per-product mean 13.92, ridge 13.13).
  • Baselines: the template's per-product mean, plus per product × store and "same week last year".
  • Models: one-hot + ridge, then gradient boosting with categorical product and store (optionally Poisson loss). A bootstrap interval resampling weeks. Errors by category, with the baseline beside them.
Key numbers (from the instructor's own run on the course data)
  • Baselines on 2025: per-product mean 13.42; per product × store 12.95; same week last year 13.81 (worse: one noisy week against a yearly average); the overall mean 17.13.
  • Ridge on one-hot + numeric: 11.29. Gradient boosting (no temperature): 8.19; Poisson loss 8.12. Learning-rate tuning by rolling folds on 2024 lands on the default 0.1 (Session 13 review handout: 8.29 with a slightly smaller feature set).
  • Units saved against the per-product mean: 5.23 (95% CI 4.91 to 5.53 resampling rows; 4.68 to 5.81 resampling the 51 weeks). Model MAE 95% CI 7.92 to 8.49. Over-forecast share 48.9% (balanced).
  • By category (baseline → model): Footwear 18.7 → 12.5, Camp Kitchen 19.3 → 11.3, Snow 13.3 → 5.8; Snow in June to August sells 0.5 units a week and is forecast at 1.5.
  • Zeros: 225 in all, 200 of them Snow in summer (real). A neighbor rule (zero with 12+ units on both sides) finds 11 suspicious zeros, 4 of them in 2025 (14 stockouts were planted).
  • The confounding trap: 40%-off weeks average 9.7 units against 20.1 at full price, because deep discounts are off-season clearance.

The answer the data support

Use the gradient-boosting forecast for store orders: it cuts the weekly error by about 5 units (8.2 against 13.4 for the per-product average, about 39% lower) at every category, with the largest absolute errors on the high-volume Footwear and Camp Kitchen lines. Limitations to state: one year of training history (one of each holiday), stockout zeros that understate demand, and a model that does not set prices (price effects are confounded with season).

Traps
  • Actual temperature as a feature. It is unknown when the order is placed. On this data it barely matters (with actual temperature 8.16; with last year's temperature for that week 8.18; without 8.19), so the honest fix costs nothing; the learner who keeps it has a Data and leakage problem regardless of the number.
  • A random split of weekly rows (the Session 13 review handout): the random-split MAE is 7.60 to 7.74, which flatters the model against the 2025 truth (8.29).
  • Stockout zeros as demand. They are outliers for a regression and they understate demand; mention them, optionally exclude them from training with a stated rule.
  • Reading price effects from raw averages (40%-off weeks sell less): season, not price. A learner who claims an elasticity without product × month controls over-claims.
  • Comparing absolute MAE across categories without volume or the baseline: Snow looks best only because it sells little.
Honest negative results (score them as well as a success)
  • Temperature adds nothing measurable once the month is in: a clean, reportable negative.
  • "Same week last year" is worse than a plain average: a baseline that sounds smart and is not.
  • Rolling folds inside one year cannot validate the season: the harness's numbers (11 to 13) are higher than the 2025 test's; saying why is Exemplary Evaluation.
Follow-up questions to ask the learner
  • "Which columns do you know on the day the order is placed?"
  • "Is every zero zero demand? How would you tell?"
  • "Does the improvement hold in every category, against the same baseline?"
What a weak submission typically gets wrong
  • Uses actual temperature, or a random split, and reports the flattering number.
  • Compares with the overall mean (17.13) instead of the template's baseline.
  • Claims "discounts reduce sales" or an elasticity from raw averages.

Answer Notes · Problem B: Fraud review queue

A sound project: steps
  • Keep the January-to-May / June split; validate inside it on May (train January to April). Accuracy is not a metric here.
  • Baselines: no model (average precision = the fraud rate) and a one-line rule (rank by velocity_24h), at the team's capacity of 1% of June (83 payments).
  • Models: scaled numerics + one-hot category + logistic regression; gradient boosting (defaults, then modest tuning); optionally IsolationForest as an unsupervised comparison.
  • One look at June: average precision with a paired bootstrap interval, frauds held at 1% (and 2%, 5% if the learner explores capacity), false alarms by account age and hour.
Key numbers (from the instructor's own run on the course data)
  • June: 8262 payments, 64 frauds; monthly fraud rate 1.0% (January) to 0.77% (June); "nothing is fraud" accuracy 0.9923.
  • June average precision / AUC / frauds in the top 1% (83 held): logistic regression 0.539 / 0.883 / 33; boosting defaults 0.477 / 0.895 / 32; tuned boosting (learning rate 0.05, 300 iterations, 15 leaves, 40 per leaf, l2 1.0, early stopping) 0.556 / 0.901 / 35; IsolationForest without labels 0.376 / 0.848 / 31. A random 1% holds 0.64 frauds; the velocity rule's top 1% holds 19 (average precision 0.250).
  • Validation on May: logistic regression 0.539, boosting defaults 0.491, tuned 0.532: the validation month does not prefer boosting.
  • Tuned boosting: AP 95% CI 0.423 to 0.668; minus defaults +0.079 (0.027 to 0.133); minus logistic regression 0.017 (-0.046 to 0.077).
  • Capacity: hold 2% → 41 frauds (recall 0.64); 5% → 47 (0.73). Lesson 2.5's costs ($10 a review, $375 a missed fraud): June costs $24,000 with no reviews, $11,705 holding 1%, $10,285 at 2%, $9,865 at the break-even probability 0.027 (199 held: more than the team's capacity).
  • Who bears the errors: new accounts (under 30 days) are 8.4% of legitimate June payments but 29.2% of the 48 false alarms; night payments 3.6% of legitimate, 29.2% of false alarms.

The answer the data support

Hold the riskiest 1% of payments for review each day: on June that catches 33 to 35 of 64 frauds with either logistic regression or tuned boosting (about half the fraud, against under one for a random 1%), and the two models cannot be told apart on one month. Recommend the simpler model unless boosting wins on more months, keep holds as reviews (never automatic declines), and give new accounts a fast release path because they bear a third of the false alarms. A capacity of about 2% would catch about two thirds of the fraud and is cheaper under the course's cost figures: a business decision worth putting to the team.

Traps
  • Accuracy (0.9923 for doing nothing).
  • A stratified random split mixing months: it hides the drift and gives a different number (tuned boosting AP 0.506, AUC 0.864); the card must use June.
  • Resampling (SMOTE, undersampling) before the split, or outside an imblearn pipeline.
  • Declaring boosting the winner from 0.556 vs 0.539 without an interval.
  • Removing the account-age feature (account_age_days) to protect new customers without measuring the effect on every group.
Honest negative results (score them as well as a success)
  • Tuned boosting is not distinguishable from logistic regression on June (difference interval includes 0), and May validation prefers neither: the Modeling row rewards saying so and choosing on other grounds.
  • About a third of frauds look like normal purchases: recall at sensible capacity tops out around two thirds. "No model catches them all" is a finding, not a failure.
  • The unsupervised IsolationForest is clearly worse (0.376): fine to report as a tried-and-rejected idea.
Follow-up questions to ask the learner
  • "How many payments can the team look at, and how many frauds does that hold?"
  • "Which month chose your model, and which month tests it?"
  • "Who is kept waiting by a false alarm, and what happens to them?"
What a weak submission typically gets wrong
  • Leads with accuracy or with AUC only.
  • Tunes on June.
  • No capacity, no subgroup analysis of the false alarms.

Answer Notes · Problem C: Review triage

A sound project: steps
  • Text only (or text + title, with the choice stated). TF-IDF + logistic regression first; vectorizer inside the pipeline; stratified CV with accuracy AND negative recall.
  • Improvements: bigrams, class_weight="balanced", or a threshold on P(negative) chosen on out-of-fold predictions for a target negative recall.
  • Test once: accuracy with an interval, negative recall, and the share of reviews flagged at the operating point. Errors with "not" vs without; by category. Read the most confident errors.
  • Measure (and caveat) what the model does with the 905 3-star reviews it never saw.
Key numbers (from the instructor's own run on the course data)
  • Test set: 774 reviews, 74.5% positive; always positive 0.745 with negative recall 0.
  • TF-IDF + LR: CV 0.857, test 0.877, negative recall 0.660; English stop words (removes "not") test 0.864; 1-2 grams 0.877 / 0.655; 1-2 grams balanced CV 0.875, test 0.873, negative recall 0.873.
  • Threshold for 90% negative recall chosen on CV: P(negative) ≥ 0.235; on the test set negative recall 0.909, 36.8% of reviews flagged, precision 0.628. Accuracy 95% CI (1-2 grams) 0.855 to 0.901.
  • Titles alone: 0.969; title + text 0.937 (only 167 distinct titles in training: short phrases that restate the rating).

The answer the data support

Use the model to order the product team's queue, not to hide anything: with balanced weights (or a threshold set for 90% negative recall) it finds about nine in ten complaints while flagging about a third of all reviews, against none for the current "read them in order". Limitations: 3-star reviews are unvalidated (a fifth to two thirds would be flagged, depending on the threshold), negation ("not waterproof at all") is the main weakness, and some star ratings contradict their own text.

Traps
  • Accuracy only (0.877 sounds good while a third of complaints are missed).
  • Stop words from scikit-learn's English list: they remove "not".
  • Fitting the vectorizer on all reviews before the split.
  • Titles without saying so: 0.969 is a near-restatement of the rating, legitimate only if titles arrive with the review; the card must state it.
  • Blaming the model for contradictory stars without reading the errors.
Honest negative results (score them as well as a success)
  • Bigrams do not help on this small vocabulary (0.877 either way): a tried-and-rejected change.
  • The accuracy interval (about ±0.02) is wider than most differences between variants: rank them by negative recall and the business cost, not by the third decimal.
Follow-up questions to ask the learner
  • "Which error is the costly one, and what is your recall of it?"
  • "What will the live system do with a 3-star review?"
  • "Read me your three most confident mistakes."
What a weak submission typically gets wrong
  • Reports accuracy without negative recall.
  • Drops 3-star reviews and never mentions them again.
  • Uses titles silently.

Answer Notes · Problem D: Recommendations

A sound project: steps
  • Keep leave-last-out for the test (3,000 customers, one hidden purchase each); carve a second hidden purchase from the training rows for validation; mask owned products everywhere.
  • Baselines: random (about 0.05) and most popular unowned (0.209). Models: item-kNN (cosine on the binary matrix), truncated SVD with k chosen on validation, content-based on primary_activity (with popularity as a tie-break).
  • Test once: hit rate at 10 with a paired bootstrap against popularity; by history length; coverage (distinct products recommended) and popularity bias.
  • Cold start in the card: what a new customer and a new product see.
Key numbers (from the instructor's own run on the course data)
  • Test hit rate at 10: random 0.047, popularity 0.209, item-kNN 0.308, SVD k = 5 0.311.

The answer the data support

Replace the best-seller row with item-based recommendations: the customer's next purchase is in the list about 31% of the time against 21%, for customers with short and long histories alike, and the list covers 161 products instead of 31. SVD is a statistical tie and harder to explain; item-kNN's "because you bought X" is easier to show. Limitations: brand-new customers still get best sellers, a never-bought product can only be reached by content (which alone is weaker than popularity), and purchase histories are personal data.

Traps
  • Choosing k on the test set (Lesson 11.2 shows the test curve for teaching; a capstone must use the validation item).
  • Forgetting the owned-product mask: 0.113 instead of 0.308.
  • Evaluating on training purchases or building the matrix from all rows.
  • Calling a 0.003 SVD gain a win.
  • Publishing example customer lists (purchase histories) in figures.
Honest negative results (score them as well as a success)
  • SVD does not beat item-kNN (interval includes 0).
  • Content-based alone loses to popularity (0.176 vs 0.209): report it, and keep it for cold start.
  • On this data the random-item split is barely different (0.299 vs 0.308) because purchase dates are spread at random; the latest-item split is still the right design, and a learner who notices this and says why scores Exemplary in Data and leakage.
Follow-up questions to ask the learner
  • "Which purchase chose your k?"
  • "What does a brand-new customer see, and a product nobody has bought?"
  • "How much of your list is best sellers?"
What a weak submission typically gets wrong
  • No mask; k from the test set.
  • Only the average hit rate: no history-length or coverage view.
  • A model card that shows individual customers' lists.

Answer Notes · Problem E: Customer segments

A sound project: steps
  • Behavior columns only (the template's four: orders_12m, avg_order_value, discount_share, return_rate), scaled; logs of the skewed two are a reasonable variant.
  • Choose k with several signals: silhouette by k, the inertia elbow, Gaussian-mixture BIC, and stability (ARI across seeds and between a model fitted on half the data and the full model).
  • Profiles per segment (means of the four features and of app_user), names a marketer recognizes, and a churn check AFTER clustering (never trained on). age_group kept out; segments checked by age afterwards.

The answer the data support

Five stable behavior segments describe customers far better than the loyalty tiers (silhouette 0.47 against 0.08) and survive another seed and half the data. They differ sharply in churn (3% to 32%) though churn was never used, which makes them useful for message planning; the card says they describe behavior, not causes, and that age was left out and checked afterwards.

Traps
  • No scaling (a higher silhouette from a clustering dominated by order value).
  • All numeric columns thrown in (silhouette drops to under 0.2).
  • k from one signal (BIC keeps falling; the elbow alone is vague).
  • Demographics in the clustering.
  • Causal names ("deal hunters leave because of discounts").
Honest negative results (score them as well as a success)
  • BIC does not pick 5: a reported disagreement between signals is good science.
  • The four-segment solution is unstable: say why k = 4 was rejected.
  • Log transforms change little (ARI 0.92 with the raw-scaled solution): a tried-and-rejected idea.
Follow-up questions to ask the learner
  • "Which column dominates your distances?"
  • "Do your segments survive another seed and half the data?"
  • "Would a marketer recognize each profile from its numbers alone?"
What a weak submission typically gets wrong
  • Silhouette only.
  • Segments named before profiles are computed.
  • Churn used in the clustering (then the churn check means nothing).
