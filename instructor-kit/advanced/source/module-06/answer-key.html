9.2 · Linear Discriminant Analysis: self-check

Q1
What does this print?
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import LinearDiscriminantAnalysis
X, y = make_blobs(n_samples=200, centers=4, n_features=2, random_state=1)
print(LinearDiscriminantAnalysis().fit(X, y).transform(X).shape)
A) (200, 3)
B) (200, 2)
C) (200, 4)
D) (200, 1)
Why: LDA gives at most min(features, classes - 1) axes: four classes would allow three, but there are only two features, so two.
A is wrong: Three would need at least three features.
C is wrong: Never one axis per class: at most classes minus one.
D is wrong: One axis is the two-class case.
Q2
What does LDA choose a direction for, compared with PCA?
A) Class means far apart relative to the spread inside each class; PCA chooses the largest total spread and never sees the labels
B) The largest total spread, like PCA, but faster
C) Curved directions where PCA uses straight ones
D) The direction of the most important single feature
Why: Fisher's criterion: between-class spread over within-class spread. The team photo: each club bunched up, the clubs far apart.
B is wrong: That is PCA's goal.
C is wrong: Both are linear; curves are Lesson 9.3.
D is wrong: An LDA axis is a weighted mix of all features.
Q3
What does this print?
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import LinearDiscriminantAnalysis
X = [[1.0], [2.0], [3.0], [9.0], [10.0]]
y = ["a", "a", "a", "b", "b"]
print(LinearDiscriminantAnalysis(priors=[0.5, 0.5]).fit(X, y).priors_)
[0.5 0.5]
Why: Priors you pass replace the class shares learned from the data (here they would be 0.6 and 0.4). Priors move the decision threshold; they never change the axis or the ranking.
Q4
LDA on ten payment categories gives nine axes, but the first carries 0.995 of the separation and leans on amount (+1.42) and items (-0.59). What is the best reading?
A) Nine useful directions: plot them all
B) The categories are perfectly separable
C) The payment details separate categories along essentially one direction, price per item, and only weakly (accuracy 0.290 against 0.219)
D) Amount and items were not scaled
Why: explained_variance_ratio_ for LDA is the share of class separation. Nearly all of it is on LD1, which is high amount with few items: an expensive single product. Six categories are never predicted. A weak, honest result.
A is wrong: Eight of the axes carry almost nothing.
B is wrong: 29% accuracy is far from separable.
D is wrong: The pipeline scaled them; the weights are comparable.
Q5
On 400 customers with 290 added noise columns and SHUFFLED churn labels, LDA fitted on all rows and then cross-validated scores AUC 0.998. Inside a pipeline it scores 0.481. Why?
A) The real columns still carry churn signal
B) kNN with 15 neighbors overfits
C) Shuffling did not work
D) LDA used every row's label, including the test folds', to build its axis; with 300 columns it can separate any labels
Why: A supervised reducer outside cross-validation turns the test labels into a feature. Inside the pipeline each fold builds its axis from its training rows only, and the score falls to chance, the truth for shuffled labels.
A is wrong: The labels were shuffled: there is no signal left to find.
B is wrong: kNN cannot invent 0.998 on random labels.
C is wrong: The honest score of 0.481 shows the shuffle worked.
Q6
You have 80 rows, 1,000 features, and want an LDA plot AND classification. Which setting?
A) LinearDiscriminantAnalysis()
B) LinearDiscriminantAnalysis(solver="eigen", shrinkage="auto")
C) LinearDiscriminantAnalysis(solver="lsqr", shrinkage="auto")
D) LinearDiscriminantAnalysis(shrinkage="auto")
Why: Far more features than rows needs shrinkage; only the eigen solver supports both shrinkage and transform.
A is wrong: No shrinkage: a noisy covariance estimate.
C is wrong: lsqr classifies but has no transform.
D is wrong: The default svd solver raises NotImplementedError with shrinkage.

9.3 · t-SNE and UMAP: self-check

Q7
On a t-SNE map, segment A is a big island and segment B a small island far away. What can you conclude?
A) A is more varied than B
B) B is very different from every other segment
C) Little about size or distance: check spread and separation in the original data
D) B is noise
Why: t-SNE keeps neighborhoods, not sizes or gaps (the three-blob demo: a seven-fold spread difference became under 1.5).
A is wrong: Map sizes are equalized by the adaptive bell curve.
B is wrong: Gaps between islands are not reliable.
D is wrong: Nothing on a map labels noise; that needs a density method in the original space.
Q8
In today's fraud-map lab, 0.630 of a fraud's map neighbors are frauds, and 0.614 of its neighbors in the original space are. What does that tell you about the colleague's "fraud is an island" map?
A) The map reproduces a neighborhood structure that exists in the data; now confirm any group in the original space (a clustering found 239 frauds together, 199 elsewhere)
B) The map created the fraud island
C) The map is wrong because the numbers differ
D) Use a lower perplexity to make the island clearer
Why: A map is trustworthy where its neighbor numbers match the original space. The claim is partly right: about half the frauds form a tight group; the rest mix with honest payments.
B is wrong: The same share exists before any map was drawn.
C is wrong: 0.630 against 0.614 is close: the map preserved it.
D is wrong: Tuning a picture until it looks clearer is how maps mislead.
Q9
What happens when this runs?
import numpy as np
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE
X = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=(12, 3))
TSNE(random_state=42).fit_transform(X)
ValueError: perplexity (30.0) must be less than n_samples (12)
Why: The default perplexity is 30, and a point cannot attend to 30 neighbors among 12 rows. Use a much smaller perplexity (well below a third of the rows) or more rows.
Q10
Each day's new customers must be placed on an existing map, and the same embedding should feed a model inside a pipeline. Which method?
A) t-SNE with a fixed random_state
B) t-SNE rerun every day on all customers
C) PCA to 2 components, because only PCA has transform
D) UMAP: fit once, then transform new rows; it can sit in a pipeline
Why: A fitted UMAP has transform (0.690 of 500 new images landed among their own class in the demo); t-SNE learns no function for new rows.
A is wrong: A seed does not give t-SNE a transform.
B is wrong: The whole layout changes every day.
C is wrong: UMAP also has transform, and keeps neighborhoods far better than a 2-component PCA view.
Q11
Why reduce 784 pixels to about 50 PCA components before t-SNE?
A) t-SNE refuses more than 50 columns
B) Speed, and the last few hundred components of image data are mostly noise
C) PCA adds the labels
D) It makes the map's axes interpretable
Why: Distances on 50 columns are much cheaper, and dropping noisy low-variance components makes neighborhoods cleaner.
A is wrong: t-SNE accepts any width, just slowly.
C is wrong: PCA never sees labels.
D is wrong: t-SNE axes never mean anything.
Q12
A t-SNE map of 200 rows at perplexity 5 shows six clumps. What do you do before telling anyone there are six groups?
A) Nothing: clumps are clusters
B) Change the seed until the clumps are clearer
C) Raise min_dist
D) Try several perplexities and confirm with a clustering and its profiles in the original space
Why: At low perplexity t-SNE makes clumps out of pure noise (the demo). Groups must be validated in the original data.
A is wrong: The pure-noise map shows clumps from nothing.
B is wrong: Seed-shopping for a picture is not evidence.
C is wrong: min_dist is a UMAP setting, and it changes packing, not truth.

The Lesson Pages' Own Quick Quizzes

Lesson 9.2 · Linear Discriminant Analysis
  1. C (300, 3): min(3 features, 5 - 1) axes.
  2. A PCA maximizes total variance; LDA between-class separation relative to within-class spread.
  3. B LDA saw the test folds' labels while building its axes.
  4. A More flagged, recall rises, ROC AUC unchanged.
  5. C solver="eigen", shrinkage="auto".
Lesson 9.3 · t-SNE and UMAP
  1. C Very little about size or distance; check those in the original data.
  2. A True False: TSNE has fit_transform but no transform.
  3. B UMAP, using transform on the new rows.
  4. A The map makes the segments look better separated than they are.
  5. C Try other perplexities and check for clusters in the original data.

Exit Ticket

  1. LDA gives at most classes minus one axes (one for two classes); PCA gives one component per column.
  2. Cluster sizes and distances between clusters (and the axes).
  3. UMAP: fit once on today's customers, then transform tomorrow's through the same fitted scaler or PCA (best as one pipeline).

Common Wrong Answers and What They Reveal

  • Q1 C ((200, 4)). One axis per class. Recite: classes minus one, and never more than features.
  • Q4 A (nine useful directions). Reads the axis count, not the separation shares.
  • Q5 A. Thinks shuffled labels keep signal. Ask what shuffling destroys.
  • Q7 A or B. Reads map sizes and gaps; the five rules again.
  • Q10 C. Thinks only PCA can transform; UMAP can.
  • Checkpoint, Part C: scaler fitted on all rows, or PC1's AUC reported as 0.369 (a flipped sign: PCA signs are arbitrary).
