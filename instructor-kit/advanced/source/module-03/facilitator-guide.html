Answers
  • W1 Fidelity (exp.score, and the surrogate's prediction close to the model) and stability (the top reasons survive other seeds).
  • W2 No evidence that recall differs by age group, and the groups are too small to rule out a moderate difference.
  • W3 16.0: clusters {0, 4} and {10, 14}, centers 2 and 12; each point is 2 away: 4 x 2² = 16.
  • W4 Distances depend on units: the column with the largest spread would decide the clusters (dollars over orders, account age over items).
  • W5 Probably nothing: cluster numbers are arbitrary names. Name clusters from their profiles.

Mental model to say

"The back room of a Pinecrest store, piled with returned gear to sort into bins. Two bins: tents next to socks. Fifty bins: identical headlamps in different bins. A good number of bins has things in the same bin alike (cohesion), bins clearly different (separation), a colleague sorting the same pile would come up with much the same bins (stability), and the bins are useful to whoever unpacks them."

Then: "No single number captures all four, so we collect evidence and look for agreement. Elbow, silhouette, stability, usefulness." Ask for the 8.1 homework: which named groups merged at k = 4 and split at k = 6?

  7. Stability: resample and refit (4m). "Real groups should not depend on exactly which customers were in the table." 
    def stability(k, n_boot=8):
    """ARI between the full-data clustering and refits on bootstrap samples."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    aris = []
    for b in range(n_boot):
        rows_b = rng.choice(len(Z), size=len(Z), replace=True)
        refit = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=5, random_state=b).fit(Z.iloc[rows_b])
        aris.append(adjusted_rand_score(fits[k].labels_, refit.predict(Z)))
    return np.mean(aris), np.min(aris)

for k in range(3, 8):
    mean_ari, worst_ari = stability(k)
    print(f"k={k}: mean ARI {mean_ari:.3f}   worst {worst_ari:.3f}")

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • One score as the answer. "The silhouette is highest at 5, done." Coach: "What do stability and the profiles say? Would marketing act differently on the groups?" Lab 8.2 A is the case where scores and usefulness pull slightly apart.
  • Comparing silhouettes across feature sets. "The log version scores 0.479, the raw 0.472, so logs are better." Coach: each score lives in its own space; choose features for business and statistical reasons, then compare k within one space.
  • Refitting preprocessing on new customers. Silent. Coach: "Which customers did this scaler learn its mean from?" The pipeline (segmenter.predict) makes the mistake impossible.

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud:

  • What are the four kinds of evidence for k? Answer: elbow, silhouette, stability under resampling, usefulness (sizes, outcomes, actions).
  • A point's silhouette is negative. What does it mean? Answer: on average it is closer to another cluster than to its own: probably misassigned or on a border.
  • Why does the ARI ignore cluster numbers? Answer: it compares pairs of points (together or apart), and the numbers are only names.
  • Why put the log, the scaler and K-Means in one pipeline? Answer: new customers then go through exactly the fitted steps; nothing is refitted on them.

Hierarchical Clustering

75 min
Objectives (from the lesson page)
  • Explain how agglomerative clustering builds a hierarchy by repeatedly merging the two closest groups
  • Read a SciPy linkage matrix and a dendrogram, and cut the tree by number of clusters or by height
  • Compare single, complete, average and Ward linkage and predict how each behaves on chains, blobs and outliers
  • Use AgglomerativeClustering with the right metric and linkage, including non-Euclidean distances
  • Judge when hierarchical clustering beats K-Means, and when its cost rules it out

Mental model to say

"Every item in the gear closet lies on its own. Find the two most similar and put them in a box. Treat the box as one item; repeat: the two closest things, items or boxes, go in a box. Keep going until everything is in one big box, writing down every merge and how far apart the two things were. That record is the hierarchy: you can open the boxes down to any level later."

On the board: n points → n − 1 merges; the dendrogram draws each merge at its height; a horizontal cut gives flat clusters; a long empty stretch in the tree is a natural answer. Then the one choice that changes everything: the linkage, the distance between two groups. And the price: every pairwise distance.

  • Old tutorial code. affinity=, both n_clusters and distance_threshold, Ward with cosine. Read the last line: each message says the fix.
  • Not printing cluster sizes. Single linkage "finds five clusters": 5,605, 392 and three single customers. Coach: "Print np.bincount(labels) every time."

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud:

  • How many rows does a linkage matrix for 200 products have? Answer: 199, one per merge.
  • Which linkage follows chains, and what is its danger? Answer: single; one bridge point glues groups together (chaining).
  • Why can Ward not use cosine distance? Answer: it merges by increase in variance, which is defined only for Euclidean distance.
  • How do you assign a new customer after agglomerative clustering? Answer: to the nearest cluster mean, or with a classifier trained on the labels; there is no predict.

Closing

Exit ticket (3 questions, no notes, 4 minutes)
  1. Name the four kinds of evidence for choosing k, and one thing each can tell you. Answer: elbow (where extra clusters stop paying off), silhouette (cohesion vs separation, per cluster too), stability (groups survive resampling, ARI), usefulness (sizes, outcomes like churn, distinct actions).
  2. Why did correlation distance find product families (by buyers, or by season) when Euclidean distance would not? Answer: Euclidean mostly measures volume or popularity; correlation compares the pattern (who buys it, when it sells) regardless of level.
  3. When would you choose K-Means over agglomerative clustering? Answer: many rows (hundreds of thousands), a need to assign new rows with predict, round groups on Euclidean features; agglomerative when you want the whole hierarchy, a non-Euclidean distance, or no random start.
