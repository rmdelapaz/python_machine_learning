Lessons

8.2

Choosing k and Customer Segments

By the end you can
  • Read an elbow plot and explain why inertia alone cannot choose k
  • Compute and interpret silhouette scores, overall and per cluster, from the formula and with scikit-learn
  • Test whether clusters are stable by refitting on resampled data and comparing labelings with the adjusted Rand index
  • Build a reusable clustering pipeline (log transform, scaling, K-Means) and use MiniBatchKMeans when data gets large
  • Profile, name and size segments, link them to an outcome such as churn, and write recommendations with honest limits
Key ideas
  • No answer key. Collect four kinds of evidence and look for agreement: elbow, silhouette, stability, usefulness.
  • Elbow. Inertia always falls as k grows; read where the drops become small, never its minimum.
  • Silhouette. s = (b - a) / max(a, b) per point; near 1 snug, near 0 on a border, negative probably misassigned. Look per cluster, not only the mean. It costs every pairwise distance: sample on big data.
  • Stability. Refit on bootstrap samples and compare with the adjusted Rand index (same groups = 1, random = about 0, numbers ignored).
  • Prepare features. Log right-skewed counts and money, then scale, all in one pipeline so new rows get the same fitted steps.
  • Segments. Profile (an index of 100 = average), name, size, link to outcomes the clustering never saw, suggest actions. Segments describe; they do not explain or target.
Worked example: the adjusted Rand index by pair counting

The ARI drives the stability check, so it is worth seeing once at full size. Two labelings of six points: the second run uses other numbers and moved one point.

from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score, rand_score
import itertools

first  = [0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1]
second = [5, 5, 7, 7, 9, 9]          # another run: other numbers, one point moved
pairs = list(itertools.combinations(range(6), 2))
agree = sum((first[a] == first[b]) == (second[a] == second[b]) for a, b in pairs)
print(f"pairs: {len(pairs)}, labelings agree on {agree}: Rand index {agree / len(pairs):.3f}")
print(f"rand_score {rand_score(first, second):.3f}   adjusted_rand_score {adjusted_rand_score(first, second):.3f}")
pairs: 15, labelings agree on 10: Rand index 0.667
rand_score 0.667   adjusted_rand_score 0.242

Of the 15 pairs of points, the two labelings agree (both "together" or both "apart") on 10: a Rand index of 0.667. That sounds good, but random labelings also agree on many pairs, mostly "apart" ones. The ADJUSTED Rand index subtracts what chance would give:

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
random_aris = [adjusted_rand_score(first, rng.integers(0, 3, size=6)) for _ in range(1000)]
print(f"ARI of random labelings: mean {np.mean(random_aris):.3f}")
ARI of random labelings: mean -0.009

Random labelings score about 0, so 0.242 is modest agreement, and the 0.98 to 0.99 of the customer segments under resampling is very strong.

8.3

Hierarchical Clustering

By the end you can
  • Explain how agglomerative clustering builds a hierarchy by repeatedly merging the two closest groups
  • Read a SciPy linkage matrix and a dendrogram, and cut the tree by number of clusters or by height
  • Compare single, complete, average and Ward linkage and predict how each behaves on chains, blobs and outliers
  • Use AgglomerativeClustering with the right metric and linkage, including non-Euclidean distances
  • Judge when hierarchical clustering beats K-Means, and when its cost rules it out
Key ideas
  • Agglomerative. Start with every point alone; merge the two closest groups; record the height; n − 1 merges in all. No random start, no k up front.
  • Linkage matrix and dendrogram. Each row: the two clusters merged, the distance, the new size. A horizontal cut gives flat clusters; a long empty stretch suggests a natural number.
  • Linkage. Single (closest pair: chains), complete (farthest pair: compact, outlier-sensitive), average (all pairs: any distance), Ward (variance increase: round groups, Euclidean only).
  • Any distance. metric= (or SciPy's pdist): correlation and cosine compare patterns, not volumes.
  • scikit-learn details. metric= not affinity=; n_clusters=None with distance_threshold; no predict.
  • Cost. Every pairwise distance: fine for thousands, impossible for millions. Sample, build the tree, assign the rest to the nearest mean.
Worked example: complete and single linkage by hand

Four points on a line: 0, 4, 5 and 11. Work out the merges by hand for complete and for single linkage, then check.

import numpy as np
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage

points = np.array([[0.0], [4.0], [5.0], [11.0]])
print("complete:\n", linkage(points, method="complete").round(1))
print("single:\n", linkage(points, method="single").round(1))
complete:
 [[ 1.  2.  1.  2.]
 [ 0.  4.  5.  3.]
 [ 3.  5. 11.  4.]]
single:
 [[1. 2. 1. 2.]
 [0. 4. 4. 3.]
 [3. 5. 6. 4.]]

Both start by merging 4 and 5 (distance 1) into cluster 4. Then they disagree on HOW FAR the box {4, 5} is: complete linkage uses the farthest member, so 0 is 5 away and 11 is 7 away; 0 joins at 5, and 11 joins last at 11 (its distance to the far end, 0). Single linkage uses the nearest member: 0 joins at 4 and 11 at 6. Same merges in the same order here, very different heights: the cut that gives two clusters lies between 5 and 11 for complete, between 4 and 6 for single.

Glossary

TermMeaning
elbowThe bend in the inertia curve where extra clusters stop paying off
silhouette(b - a) / max(a, b) per point: cohesion against separation, from -1 to 1
adjusted Rand index (ARI)Agreement between two labelings over pairs of points, corrected for chance; ignores cluster numbers
bootstrapA sample of the same size drawn with replacement, to test stability
segmentA profiled, named cluster with a size, linked outcomes and an action
agglomerative clusteringBottom-up merging of the two closest groups, n - 1 times
linkage matrixSciPy's record of the merges: the two clusters, their distance, the new size
dendrogramThe tree drawing of the merges; a horizontal cut gives flat clusters
linkageHow the distance between two groups is defined: single, complete, average, Ward
chainingSingle linkage gluing groups together through a trail of close points
correlation distance1 minus the correlation of two rows: 0 same pattern, 1 unrelated, 2 opposite
cophenetic correlationHow well the tree's merge heights preserve the original distances
