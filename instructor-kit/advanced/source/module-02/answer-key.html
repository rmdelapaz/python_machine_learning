7.3 · LIME and Fairness Checks: self-check

Q1
In Lab 7.3 A, LIME explains a fraud payment the model scores at 0.990. The explanation has fidelity 0.12 and a surrogate prediction of 0.077. What should you do?
A) Treat it as unreliable: the flat map does not imitate the model near this payment; report SHAP's reasons, or fewer reasons
B) Report it: fidelity only matters for regression models
C) Trust the surrogate: the payment's real risk is 0.077
D) Raise num_features until fidelity reaches 0.9, then report every rule
Why: Fidelity (the surrogate's weighted R²) and the surrogate's own prediction are LIME's built-in honesty checks. Here both fail: almost every random variant of a payment looks legitimate when fraud is 0.88% of the data, so the straight line is fitted to a flat neighborhood. SHAP's TreeExplainer is exact for this model and agrees on velocity as the lead reason.
B is wrong: Fidelity is about how well the surrogate imitates the model, for any kind of model.
C is wrong: The surrogate is an approximation of the model; the model's 0.990 is the prediction being explained.
D is wrong: More features raise the fit a little (Lesson 7.3 Exercise 1) and make the explanation useless for a person.
Q2
What does this print?
import numpy as np
width = 0.75 * np.sqrt(4)
print(np.exp(-np.array([0.0, 1.5, 3.0]) ** 2 / width ** 2).round(3))
[1. 0.368 0.018]
Why: LIME's kernel weight is exp(-distance² / width²). With 4 features the default width is 0.75 × 2 = 1.5: a variant at distance 0 weighs 1, at one width (1.5) e^-1 = 0.368, at two widths e^-4 = 0.018. Variants far from the customer barely count.
Q3
A what-if check sets C104051's days since the last order to 60 and the model still predicts 0.645. What is the best lesson from this?
A) The model is broken: recency is its top driver
B) Getting C104051 to order will not keep them
C) A single top reason hides the rest: discounts and tickets still hold the risk up, and the what-if describes the model, not the customer
D) LIME was wrong to rank recency first
Why: What-if checks ask the model a counterfactual question. The answer shows that "make them order" would not, according to the model, make this customer safe. It says nothing about what the customer would really do: that needs an experiment.
A is wrong: Recency still matters: 364 days gives 0.972, 120 days 0.619.
B is wrong: That is a claim about the world; the what-if only describes the model's answer.
D is wrong: LIME's binned rule (more than 110 days) and the what-if answer different questions.
Q4
Pinecrest's offer is a benefit: a churner who is flagged gets a discount, one who is missed does not. Which comparison across age groups matches that harm?
A) Flag rate (demographic parity)
B) Recall among churners (equal opportunity)
C) Precision among the flagged
D) Accuracy
Why: The harm is a churner who misses out on an offer. Recall asks, for each group's real churners, what share were flagged. Lesson 7.3 compares exactly that (54% to 77%, and a permutation test says the gap is consistent with chance).
A is wrong: Equal flag rates can coexist with very different chances for real churners to be reached.
C is wrong: Precision measures wasted offers within a group: the cost side, not the missed-benefit side.
D is wrong: Accuracy mixes both errors and is dominated by the many non-churners.
Q5
What does this print?
import pandas as pd
audit = pd.DataFrame({"group": ["A", "A", "A", "B", "B", "B", "B"],
                      "churned": [1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0],
                      "flag":    [1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0]})
churners = audit[audit["churned"] == 1]
print(churners.groupby("group")["flag"].mean().round(2).tolist())
[0.5, 0.67]
Why: Recall by group is the flag rate among each group's churners only. Group A has two churners, one flagged: 0.5. Group B has three churners, two flagged: 0.67. The non-churner in A who was flagged does not enter the recall at all.
Q6
Lab 7.3 B finds the model over-predicts churn for 45 to 54 year-olds by 2.5 points and under-predicts for 35 to 44 by 4.2 points; 64% of shuffles produce a gap at least as large. What do you report?
A) The model is miscalibrated against older customers and must be retrained per age group
B) The model is proven equally calibrated for every age group
C) Age should be added as a feature to fix the calibration
D) No evidence of a real calibration difference at this sample size; the groups are small, so keep monitoring
Why: Gaps this size are what chance produces with groups of 128 to 309 customers. The honest report has both halves: no evidence of a problem, and not enough data to rule out a moderate one.
A is wrong: A gap that shuffled labels match 64% of the time is not evidence of anything.
B is wrong: Failing to find a difference is not proof that none exists.
C is wrong: Age is used only to audit; feeding it to the model is a shortcut the lesson warns against.

7.3 · LIME and Fairness Checks: predict, then run

P1
Predict the output, then run it.
import numpy as np
width = 0.75 * np.sqrt(11)                     # LIME's default kernel width for 11 features
distance = np.array([0.0, 2.0, 5.0])           # in standard deviations from the customer
print(np.exp(-distance ** 2 / width ** 2).round(3))
[1. 0.524 0.018]
Why: Weight = exp(-d² / width²) with width 0.75 × √11 = 2.49: the customer itself weighs 1, a variant two standard deviations away about half, one five away almost nothing.
P2
Predict the output, then run it.
import pandas as pd
audit = pd.DataFrame({"age": ["young"] * 4 + ["older"] * 4,
                      "churned": [1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0],
                      "flag":    [1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0]})
print(audit[audit["churned"] == 1].groupby("age")["flag"].mean().round(2).to_dict())
{'older': 0.67, 'young': 0.5}
Why: Recall per group counts churners only: the young group's two churners include one flagged (0.5); the older group's three include two flagged (0.67). The young non-churner who was flagged does not count.

8.1 · K-Means Clustering: self-check

Q7
What does this print?
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit([[1], [3], [10], [14]])
print(km.inertia_)
10.0
Why: Two clusters, {1, 3} with center 2 and {10, 14} with center 12. Inertia adds the squared distances to the own center: 1 + 1 + 4 + 4 = 10.
Q8
Lab 8.1 A clusters 50,000 payments on amount, account age, items and card velocity without scaling. The four groups differ only in account age. Why?
A) Account age is the most important fraud signal
B) K-Means sorts by the first column with many values
C) The other three columns have too many ties
D) Distances are dominated by the column with the largest spread: account age varies by about 700 days, items by about 1
Why: K-Means measures straight-line distance, so one day of account age counts as much as one whole item. Logging the skewed columns and standardizing gives every column a say; then the groups become brand-new accounts, busy cards, big baskets and small everyday orders.
A is wrong: K-Means never sees the fraud column; the dominance is about units, not importance.
B is wrong: Column order does not matter to K-Means.
C is wrong: Ties are not the issue; spread is.
Q9
What does KMeans(n_clusters=5, n_init=10, random_state=42) do with n_init=10?
A) Runs the whole algorithm from ten starts and keeps the run with the lowest inertia
B) Runs ten rounds of assign and move, then stops
C) Tries k from 1 to 10 and keeps the best k
D) Averages the centers of ten runs
Why: One run can end in a local minimum (the lesson's random starts reached inertias from 565 to 8,423). Ten starts with k-means++ seeding make a poor final answer unlikely; the course writes n_init=10 whenever the result matters.
B is wrong: That is max_iter (the number of rounds allowed in one run).
C is wrong: K-Means never chooses k; Lesson 8.2 does that with evidence.
D is wrong: Centers from different runs are not averaged: cluster numbers do not even match between runs.
Q10
The segmentation was fitted on scaled data. Three customers sign up next month. How do you assign them?
A) Fit a new scaler on the three customers, then predict
B) Pass the fitted scaler's transform of their rows to the fitted model's predict
C) Refit K-Means on all customers plus the three
D) predict on their raw, unscaled rows
Why: New rows must be measured against the customer base the model learned from: the same fitted scaler, then the same centers. Lesson 8.2 puts both in one pipeline so that predict does it in one call.
A is wrong: Three rows scaled against each other land in the wrong segments (Lesson 8.2's Common Mistake 4).
C is wrong: Refitting can move borders and renumber clusters every month.
D is wrong: The centers live in scaled units; raw dollars would all look enormous.
Q11
What does this print?
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
scaler = StandardScaler().fit([[2.0], [4.0], [6.0]])
print(scaler.inverse_transform([[1.5]]).round(3))
[[6.449]]
Why: The scaler learned mean 4 and (population) standard deviation 1.633. inverse_transform undoes the scaling: 4 + 1.5 × 1.633 = 6.449. That is how a scaled cluster center becomes a center in real units.
Q12
In Lab 8.1 A, the "Busy cards" group has a fraud rate of 2.13% and holds 243 of the 438 frauds; the clustering never saw is_fraud. Which statement is right?
A) Busy cards is a fraud detector: flag the whole group
B) The groups differ in fraud, which shows they capture real behavior, but 97.9% of busy-card payments are legitimate: a cluster describes, it does not detect
C) The clustering leaked the fraud labels
D) The groups are meaningless because none of them is mostly fraud
Why: An outcome the clustering never saw differing between groups is evidence the groups are real. A useful description is not a classifier: flagging 11,401 payments to catch 243 frauds would swamp the review team. Lesson 8.6 detects anomalies properly.
A is wrong: That would flag 11,158 legitimate payments.
C is wrong: is_fraud was never in X.
D is wrong: Groups need not be mostly fraud to be meaningful; they describe how payments look.

8.1 · K-Means Clustering: predict, then run

P3
Predict the output, then run it.
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit([[0], [2], [10], [12]])
print(sorted(km.cluster_centers_.ravel().tolist()), km.inertia_)
[1.0, 11.0] 4.0
Why: Two clusters {0, 2} and {10, 12}, centers 1 and 11; each point is 1 from its center, so inertia 4 × 1² = 4.0.
P4
Predict the output, then run it.
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit([[0], [2], [10], [12]])
print(km.predict([[5], [7]]) == km.predict([[1], [11]]))
[ True True]
Why: 5 is nearer 1 than 11 and 7 is nearer 11 than 1, so 5 lands with 1 and 7 with 11: both comparisons are True.

The Lesson Pages' Own Quick Quizzes

Lesson 7.3 · LIME and Fairness Checks
  1. C A simple weighted linear model fitted to the original model's scores on nearby variants.
  2. A Treat it as unreliable: the surrogate does not imitate the model near this customer.
  3. B SHAP measures the share of the gap from an average customer; the slope measures sensitivity nearby, and the curve is flat at 364 days.
  4. C Outcomes can still differ by age through proxy features, so measure them by group.
  5. B No evidence of a real difference, but the groups are too small to prove there is none; keep monitoring.
Lesson 8.1 · K-Means Clustering
  1. B 1.0: clusters {1, 2} and {10, 11}, each point 0.5 from its center: 4 × 0.25.
  2. A Spend decides almost alone, because its numbers are much larger.
  3. C Some starts led to local minima; use k-means++ starts and several runs.
  4. C Probably nothing: cluster numbers are arbitrary and can come back shuffled.
  5. A Two interlocking half-moons.

Exit Ticket

  1. LIME's two checks: fidelity (exp.score; and exp.local_pred close to the model, at least on the same side of the threshold) and stability (rerun with other random_state values; the top reasons should hold).
  2. Audit by age: other features can be proxies for age, so outcomes can still differ; for a missed benefit, compare recall by group, with group sizes and a permutation test.
  3. Scaling and n_init: distances depend on units, so the column with the largest spread would decide the clusters; n_init=10 runs from ten starts and keeps the lowest inertia, guarding against local minima.

Common Wrong Answers and What They Reveal

  • Q1 C ("the real risk is 0.077"). Confuses the surrogate with the model. The model's prediction is what is being explained.
  • Q4 A (flag rate). Equal treatment in the sense of equal numbers, not equal chances for the people the offer is for. Use worked example 2.
  • Q6 A or B. Reads a non-significant gap as either a verdict or a certificate. Rehearse the two-part sentence.
  • Q7 written as 2.5 or 4.0. Forgot to square, or averaged instead of summing.
  • Q9 B ("ten rounds"). Confuses n_init with max_iter.
  • Q12 A. Treats a descriptive group as a classifier: ask how many legitimate payments would be flagged.
