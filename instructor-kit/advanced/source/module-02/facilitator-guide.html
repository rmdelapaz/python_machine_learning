Session 2: Local Explanations, Fairness and K-Means

How to teach the second session of Advanced Machine Learning: explain one prediction with a flat local map (LIME), learn when not to trust it, and audit the churn campaign for fairness across age groups (7.3); then leave labels behind and find customer groups with K-Means, learning the algorithm, its pitfalls and why scaling is a choice (8.1).

The #1 risk of Session 2: fairness becomes a debate, or a verdict

The age-group table invites strong reactions ("the model discriminates against young people!") and equally strong dismissals ("the model never sees age, so it is fair"). Both skip the lesson. Keep the room on the procedure: which harm, which metric, how many people per group, is the gap bigger than chance, what was not checked. The permutation test is the hinge: practice saying "no evidence of a difference, and not enough data to rule one out" before the session. Do not let the audit drift into whether Pinecrest should collect age at all; park it for the journal prompt.

Answers
  • W1 One more standard deviation of discount share multiplies the odds of churn by e^0.68, about 2.0, other features held fixed.
  • W2 How much the model's held-out score depends on that feature (permutation importance); on training rows memorized noise would look important too.
  • W3 3.55 0.972: base + shares = 3.55 log-odds; expit(3.55) = 0.972, C104051's churn probability.
  • W4 It is measured on training data and favors many-valued columns: in Lesson 7.1 it ranked a column of pure noise sixth.
  • W5 "No order in 240 days": a neutral fact about the account. The other turns a model's association into a claim about the person's motives.

Mental model to say

"The Earth is round, but a map of your neighborhood can be flat and still be accurate, because you only use it nearby. LIME draws a flat map of the model around one customer: make thousands of nearby variants, ask the model about each, weight them by closeness, fit a straight line. Useful for 'what pushes this customer's risk around?'; misleading if you carry it across the country."

Then the second half, a different question entirely: "Explanations tell you how a model decides. A fairness check asks whether its decisions land differently on different groups of people. Not using a sensitive attribute guarantees nothing; you have to measure, and measure honestly when groups are small."

  2. LIME by hand: the flat map (5m). "Make 3,000 variants of this customer, ask the model about each, weight each by how close it is, fit a weighted straight line. The line's slopes are the explanation." Walk the four numbered comments. 
    from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
sd = X_train.std()

# 1. variants near the customer, kept inside the training range
noise = rng.normal(size=(3000, len(features)))
variants = pd.DataFrame(x0.to_numpy() + noise * sd.to_numpy(), columns=features)
variants = variants.clip(lower=X_train.min(), upper=X_train.max(), axis=1)
variants["app_user"] = (variants["app_user"] > 0.5).astype(float)

# 2. ask the model
scores = model.predict_proba(variants)[:, 1]

# 3. weight by closeness (distance in standard deviations)
steps = (variants - x0) / sd
distance = np.sqrt((steps ** 2).sum(axis=1))
width = 0.75 * np.sqrt(len(features))        # LIME's default kernel width
weights = np.exp(-distance ** 2 / width ** 2)

# 4. fit a simple weighted model: its slopes are the explanation
surrogate = Ridge(alpha=1.0).fit(steps, scores, sample_weight=weights)
local = pd.Series(surrogate.coef_, index=features).sort_values(key=abs, ascending=False)
print(local.round(3).head(5))
print(f"fidelity (weighted R2): {surrogate.score(steps, scores, sample_weight=weights):.3f}")
print(f"surrogate at the customer: {surrogate.intercept_:.3f}   model: {proba[i]:.3f}")

    recall = frame.groupby("age")["flag"].mean()
    return recall.max() - recall.min()

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Trusting a LIME explanation without its two checks. Coach: "What is exp.score? Is exp.local_pred on the same side of your threshold as the model? Does the top reason survive another seed?" The fraud lab makes this vivid: fidelity 0.12, surrogate 0.077 for a 0.990 payment.
  • "We removed age, so the model is fair." Coach with the audit table: the model never saw age, and recall still varies from 54% to 77%. Whether that is chance is a separate question; you only know because you measured.
  • A gap read as a verdict, or a non-significant test read as proof. Coach the two-part sentence: "no evidence of a difference" AND "groups too small to rule one out". Ask for group sizes every time a rate is quoted.

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud:

  • What are LIME's four steps? Answer: perturb, predict, weight by closeness, fit a simple weighted model.
  • Why can LIME's top slope differ from SHAP's top share? Answer: the slope is sensitivity nearby; the share is the contribution to the gap from a typical customer. At 364 days the curve is flat, so the slope is small and the share large.
  • Which fairness view matches "a churner who misses out on an offer"? Answer: recall by group (equal opportunity).
  • What does a permutation test of a recall gap shuffle? Answer: the group labels among the churners, so group has nothing to do with being flagged.

Mental model to say

"Every model so far learned from examples with an answer. Marketing now asks what kinds of customers Pinecrest has, and nobody has labeled them. Picture a campground at night: hundreds of hikers and k campfires lit at random spots. Each hiker walks to the nearest fire. Each fire is moved to the middle of its crowd. Some hikers are now closer to another fire and walk over; the fires move again. After a few rounds nobody switches."

Then on the board: fires = centroids; walk to the nearest = assign; move to the middle = update; k = the number of fires, you choose it (Lesson 8.2 asks how). The one number each round lowers: inertia, the total squared distance to the own center. And the warning: "K-Means always answers, even in data with no groups. Checking the answer is your job."

  4. Lloyd's loop in fifteen lines (5m). "Assign, move, repeat until nothing moves." Narrate the broadcasting line once: every point minus every center at once, shape (points, k, features). 
    def lloyd(X, centers, max_steps=20):
    """Plain K-Means: assign each point to its nearest center, move each center to its points' mean."""
    history = [centers]
    for _ in range(max_steps):
        sq_dist = ((X[:, None, :] - centers[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2)   # shape (n_points, k)
        labels = sq_dist.argmin(axis=1)                                      # step 1: assign
        new_centers = np.array([X[labels == j].mean(axis=0) for j in range(len(centers))])  # step 2: move
        history.append(new_centers)
        if np.allclose(new_centers, centers):                                # nothing moved: converged
            break
        centers = new_centers
    return labels, centers, history

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)     # a seed whose random start is clearly poor, so there is something to watch

labels, centers, history = lloyd(X_toy, start)

my_inertia = ((X_toy - centers[labels]) ** 2).sum()

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Clustering unscaled columns. No error; the biggest numbers decide. Coach: "Read the centers in real units. Does every column vary across clusters?" Lab 8.1 B shows it on recency, spend and tickets; Lab 8.1 A on payments.
  • Reading scaled centers as real values. "0.44 orders of -0.41 dollars." Coach: inverse_transform, or simply a groupby of the original columns by label.
  • Treating cluster numbers as names. Code that hard-codes "cluster 4" breaks when the seed or the data change (debug_kmeans.py has exactly this bug). Coach: name clusters from their profiles.
