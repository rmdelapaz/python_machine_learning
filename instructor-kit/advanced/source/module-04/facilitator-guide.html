Session 4: Density-Based Clustering and Gaussian Mixtures

How to teach the fourth session of Advanced Machine Learning. Learners leave Session 3 able to give every customer one segment; today adds two honest answers: "fits nowhere" (noise, from DBSCAN, OPTICS and HDBSCAN) and "partly this, partly that" (probabilities, from Gaussian mixtures). They find dense payment patterns whose misfits hold most of the frauds, and soft segments whose confident members churn at very different rates.

The #1 risk of Session 4: believing a cluster because an algorithm produced it

Both lessons, and both labs, contain a cluster that is not a segment: customers or payments stacked on a whole-number value (one order, exactly two items) look like the densest crowd in the data. A learner who reports "HDBSCAN found 8 kinds of payment" without reading the profile table has learned the API and missed the lesson. Ask "what do the members of this cluster have in common, besides being in it?" at every profile table today. The second risk is quieter: treating predict as the whole answer of a mixture. Every mixture output today goes with its confidence.

Mental model to say

"Look down at a region at night from a plane. Towns are where the lights are packed close together, and their shapes follow rivers and valleys, not circles. Between them are dark fields with a lone farmhouse here and there. You did not need to be told how many towns there are: you counted the bright patches. And you would not call a farmhouse a town."

Then the contrast with Session 3: "K-Means and hierarchical clustering put EVERY customer in some cluster and assume roughly round blobs. Density methods give two new answers: clusters of any shape, and noise, label -1, for points that belong nowhere. The number of clusters comes out of the data."

Name the three algorithms in one breath: DBSCAN (one radius, easy to reason about), OPTICS (every radius at once, a picture), HDBSCAN (keeps the clusters that are stable across densities: the one to reach for).

Live demo (you type, they follow)

New notebook lesson_8_4. Steps 1 to 7 are the lesson's sections in order; 8 to 10 are its Common Mistakes. Ask for a prediction before every sweep.

  1. K-Means cannot follow a moon (3m). "Two interleaving half-moons. K-Means draws straight boundaries between centers; DBSCAN follows the crowd. Scale first: eps is a distance." Show the lesson's side-by-side figure after the run. 
    import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, DBSCAN
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

def label_counts(labels):
    """Cluster sizes as a dict; the key -1 is noise."""
    return pd.Series(labels).value_counts().sort_index().to_dict()

X_moons, y_moons = make_moons(n_samples=400, noise=0.08, random_state=42)
Z_moons = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X_moons)

km_labels = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit_predict(Z_moons)
db = DBSCAN(eps=0.3, min_samples=5).fit(Z_moons)

print("K-Means:", label_counts(km_labels), " ARI", round(adjusted_rand_score(y_moons, km_labels), 3))
print("DBSCAN :", label_counts(db.labels_), " ARI", round(adjusted_rand_score(y_moons, db.labels_), 3))
    K-Means: {0: 200, 1: 200}  ARI 0.475
DBSCAN : {-1: 1, 0: 200, 1: 199}  ARI 0.995
    The adjusted Rand index is back from Lesson 8.2 (today's warm-up): 1 means the true moons. Ask: "Did I tell DBSCAN there were two clusters?" (No: the count came out of the data.)
  2. Core, border, noise (2m). "Every point gets a role. Core: at least min_samples within eps, itself included. Border: not crowded itself, but next to a core point. Noise: neither, label -1." Draw the decision diagram from the lesson. 
    is_core = np.zeros(len(Z_moons), dtype=bool)
is_core[db.core_sample_indices_] = True
is_noise = db.labels_ == -1
print("core  :", is_core.sum())
print("border:", (~is_core & ~is_noise).sum())
print("noise :", is_noise.sum())
    core  : 398
border: 1
noise : 1
  3. eps is unforgiving (3m). "Too small: crumbs and noise. Too large: one blob." Ask for a prediction for eps 0.1 and eps 1.0 before running. 
    for eps in [0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0]:
    labels = DBSCAN(eps=eps, min_samples=5).fit_predict(Z_moons)
    n_clusters = len(set(labels) - {-1})
    print(f"eps={eps:<4} clusters={n_clusters:<3} noise={(labels == -1).sum():<4} ARI={adjusted_rand_score(y_moons, labels):.3f}")
    eps=0.1  clusters=29  noise=157  ARI=0.031
eps=0.2  clusters=5   noise=5    ARI=0.886
eps=0.3  clusters=2   noise=1    ARI=0.995
eps=0.5  clusters=2   noise=0    ARI=1.000
eps=1.0  clusters=1   noise=0    ARI=0.000
    The useful range is 0.3 to 0.5. On real data there is no ARI to check against, which is why the next step exists.
  4. A starting eps from the data (3m). "For each point: the distance to its min_samples-th neighbor, itself included. That is the smallest eps that makes it core. Sort, plot, look for the knee." Show the lesson's k-distance plot. 
    from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors
min_samples = 5
nn = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=min_samples).fit(Z_moons)
distances, _ = nn.kneighbors(Z_moons)        # column 0 is each point itself, at distance 0
k_dist = np.sort(distances[:, -1])
print("k-distance percentiles (50, 90, 99):", np.percentile(k_dist, [50, 90, 99]).round(3))
print("share that would be core at eps=0.3:", (k_dist <= 0.3).mean())
    k-distance percentiles (50, 90, 99): [0.112 0.186 0.278]
share that would be core at eps=0.3: 0.995
    Say the warning card: on real data the knee is a gentle curve. Treat it as the center of a short sweep.
  5. When density varies (3m). "Two tight groups close together, one loose group further away, a sprinkle of noise. True sizes 200, 200, 300. Find an eps that gets all three." 
    from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
X_blobs, y_blobs = make_blobs(n_samples=[200, 200, 300], centers=[[0, 0], [2, 0], [6, 4]],
                              cluster_std=[0.3, 0.3, 1.3], random_state=42)
X_var = np.vstack([X_blobs, rng.uniform(-3, 10, size=(30, 2))])
y_var = np.concatenate([y_blobs, np.full(30, -1)])     # -1 marks the sprinkled points
for eps in [0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 1.0]:
    labels = DBSCAN(eps=eps, min_samples=10).fit_predict(X_var)
    print(f"eps={eps:<4} {label_counts(labels)}")
    eps=0.2  {-1: 361, 0: 189, 1: 180}
eps=0.4  {-1: 180, 0: 199, 1: 202, 2: 149}
eps=0.6  {-1: 60, 0: 403, 1: 267}
eps=1.0  {-1: 20, 0: 403, 1: 307}
    No eps works: small radii lose the loose group, large radii merge the tight ones. The gap between the tight groups is narrower than the spacing inside the loose one.
  6. OPTICS: every eps at once (3m). "OPTICS records, for every point, roughly the eps at which it would be reached. Plotted in visiting order, clusters are valleys." Show the reachability plot; point at the higher valley (the loose group). 
    from sklearn.cluster import OPTICS
optics = OPTICS(min_samples=10, xi=0.1, min_cluster_size=0.05).fit(X_var)
print("OPTICS:", label_counts(optics.labels_), " ARI", round(adjusted_rand_score(y_var, optics.labels_), 3))
    OPTICS: {-1: 18, 0: 200, 1: 203, 2: 309}  ARI 0.959
    Running DBSCAN = drawing one horizontal line across this plot. One line cannot cut a high valley and two low ones well.
  7. HDBSCAN: the practical default (3m). "Build the whole hierarchy over every eps, keep the clusters that live longest. The setting you can reason about: the smallest group you care about." Mention why copy=True (scikit-learn 1.9 FutureWarning). 
    from sklearn.cluster import HDBSCAN
hdb = HDBSCAN(min_cluster_size=20, copy=True).fit(X_var)
print("HDBSCAN:", label_counts(hdb.labels_), " ARI", round(adjusted_rand_score(y_var, hdb.labels_), 3))
print("membership strength of the first 5 points:", hdb.probabilities_[:5].round(2))
    HDBSCAN: {-1: 30, 0: 302, 1: 197, 2: 201}  ARI 0.95
membership strength of the first 5 points: [1.   0.55 0.36 0.93 0.65]
    probabilities_: 1 is the dense core, near 0 the fringe, noise 0. Put the lesson's method table on the board now; it is the summary of the block.
  8. Mistake: counting noise as a cluster (1m). "No error, wrong number." 
    labels_04 = DBSCAN(eps=0.4, min_samples=10).fit_predict(X_var)
print("len(set(labels)):", len(set(labels_04)))
print("real clusters    :", len(set(labels_04) - {-1}))
    len(set(labels)): 4
real clusters    : 3
  9. Mistake: asking DBSCAN to predict (1m). "These models describe the data they saw." 
    db.predict(Z_moons[:3])
    AttributeError: 'DBSCAN' object has no attribute 'predict'
    Fix: refit, or a nearest-neighbor assignment (Exercise 4). Gaussian mixtures, after the break, can predict.
  10. Mistake: forgetting to scale, on real customers (3m). "Dollars against fractions: a radius of 0.5 means 'within 50 cents of order value'." 
    customers_raw = pd.read_csv("data/customers_churn.csv")
raw = customers_raw[["orders_12m", "avg_order_value", "discount_share", "return_rate"]]
raw_labels = DBSCAN(eps=0.5, min_samples=10).fit_predict(raw)

    clusters: 57  noise share: 0.776

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Counting -1 as a cluster. Symptom: "HDBSCAN found 8 clusters" when it found 7 and noise. Coach: "Print set(labels). Which of those is not a cluster?" Make them report noise as its own number every time.
  • Sweeping eps forever on real data. Symptom: a learner in the lab tries eps 0.55, 0.6, 0.65 hoping for a clean answer. Coach: "Do the k-distances show a knee? Do the clusters have different densities?" When no eps works, that is the finding, and the cue for HDBSCAN.
  • Trusting a cluster without its profile. Symptom: naming lab clusters "one-item shoppers" and "two-item shoppers" as segments. Coach: "What else differs between those clusters?" (Little.) Whole-number columns make shelves.

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab:

  • Why do we scale before DBSCAN? Answer: eps is a distance; columns in large units would decide it alone.
  • What does a point with label -1 mean? Answer: noise: not in any crowd. It is a result to report, not a cluster.
  • Why can no single eps find the tight and the loose groups? Answer: the gap between the tight groups is narrower than the spacing inside the loose one; any radius that connects the loose group bridges the gap.
  • How do you label a new customer with a fitted HDBSCAN? Answer: you cannot predict: refit, or assign to the nearest clustered neighbor's segment and call it noise when it is too far.

Mental model to say

"Every customer's behavior is a dab of paint, and all the paint on the palette was mixed from a few base colors. A Gaussian mixture works out the base colors (the components), how much of the palette each supplied (the weights), and, for any dab, how likely it is to have come from each color. A dab between two colors gets a split answer, and that split is information, not indecision."

Write under the three headings on the board: means_ (where), covariances_ (spread and tilt: an ellipse), weights_ (share). Then: "K-Means is close to a special case: tiny, equal, round components and all-or-nothing answers. A mixture can tilt, stretch, share, and predict for new customers, which the density methods before the break could not."

Live demo (you type, they follow)

New notebook lesson_8_5. Steps 1 to 6 follow the lesson's sections; 7 to 9 are its mistakes.

  1. One feature, two hidden groups (3m). "Six hundred values from one bell curve, four hundred from another. The model never sees which is which." Remind them: scikit-learn wants 2-D X, hence the reshape. 
    import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
group_a = rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=1.0, size=600)
group_b = rng.normal(loc=4.0, scale=1.5, size=400)
x = np.concatenate([group_a, group_b]).reshape(-1, 1)   # scikit-learn wants a 2-D X
gm_1d = GaussianMixture(n_components=2, random_state=42).fit(x)
print("means  :", gm_1d.means_.ravel().round(2))
print("stds   :", np.sqrt(gm_1d.covariances_.ravel()).round(2))
print("weights:", gm_1d.weights_.round(3))
    means  : [0.03 4.16]

weights: [0.629 0.371]
    True values: means 0 and 4, spreads 1 and 1.5, weights 60/40. Recovered from the mixed-up numbers alone.
  2. Soft membership (3m). "predict_proba: one row per value, one column per component, each row adds up to 1." Ask the room to predict the row for 2.0 before running.

  3. Ellipses, not circles (3m). "Three blobs stretched the same way. K-Means slices across them; a full-covariance component bends to each shape." 
    from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score
X_round, y_true = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=3, random_state=170)
X_stretched = X_round @ np.array([[0.6, -0.6], [-0.4, 0.8]])
km_labels = KMeans(n_clusters=3, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit_predict(X_stretched)
gm_full = GaussianMixture(n_components=3, covariance_type="full", random_state=42).fit(X_stretched)
gm_labels = gm_full.predict(X_stretched)
print("K-Means ARI:", round(adjusted_rand_score(y_true, km_labels), 3))
print("GMM ARI    :", round(adjusted_rand_score(y_true, gm_labels), 3))
    K-Means ARI: 0.577
GMM ARI    : 1.0
    Draw the EM loop from the lesson while it runs: E-step shares out each point, M-step refits each component; repeat until the log-likelihood stops improving.
  4. Covariance types, judged by BIC (4m). "BIC rewards fit and charges for every parameter. Lower is better. It never sees the true labels." 
    for cov in ["spherical", "diag", "tied", "full"]:
    gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=3, covariance_type=cov, random_state=42).fit(X_stretched)
    ari = adjusted_rand_score(y_true, gm.predict(X_stretched))
    print(f"{cov:10s} BIC {gm.bic(X_stretched):8.1f}   ARI {ari:.3f}")
    spherical  BIC   4019.6   ARI 0.568
diag       BIC   3988.1   ARI 0.516
tied       BIC   3032.8   ARI 1.000
full       BIC   3061.9   ARI 1.000
    tied wins because the groups really share one shape; full also gets them right but pays for two covariances it does not need. The axis-aligned types cannot tilt.
  5. How many components (3m). "Same tool, different question." 
    for k in range(1, 7):
    gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=k, covariance_type="tied", random_state=42).fit(X_stretched)
    print(f"k={k}  BIC {gm.bic(X_stretched):8.1f}   AIC {gm.aic(X_stretched):8.1f}")
    k=1  BIC   4272.7   AIC   4250.7
k=2  BIC   3825.1   AIC   3789.9
k=3  BIC   3032.8   AIC   2984.5
k=4  BIC   3053.1   AIC   2991.6
k=5  BIC   3072.4   AIC   2997.6
k=6  BIC   3087.2   AIC   2999.2
    A clean minimum at 3 on toy data. Warn: on the customers (the project) BIC keeps creeping down; read where the big drops stop.
  6. A mixture is a density model (3m). "score_samples is the log of the model's density. High in the middle of a component, very low far from everything." 
    gm_tied = GaussianMixture(n_components=3, covariance_type="tied", random_state=42).fit(X_stretched)
typical_point = gm_tied.means_[:1]                  # the center of one component
odd_point = np.array([[0.0, -20.0]])                # far from every group
print("log-density, center of a component:", gm_tied.score_samples(typical_point).round(2))
print("log-density, far-away point       :", gm_tied.score_samples(odd_point).round(2))
print("lowest log-density among the data :", gm_tied.score_samples(X_stretched).min().round(2))
    log-density, center of a component: [-1.47]
log-density, far-away point       : [-1397.33]
lowest log-density among the data : -7.94
    Compare log-densities within one model only. This is the anomaly score of Lesson 8.6.
  7. Mistake: component numbers mean nothing (3m). "Two seeds, the same clustering, different numbers." 
    run_a = GaussianMixture(n_components=3, covariance_type="tied", random_state=42).fit(X_stretched)
run_b = GaussianMixture(n_components=3, covariance_type="tied", random_state=7).fit(X_stretched)
print("first labels, run A:", run_a.predict(X_stretched)[:8])
print("first labels, run B:", run_b.predict(X_stretched)[:8])
print("ARI between runs   :", adjusted_rand_score(run_a.predict(X_stretched), run_b.predict(X_stretched)))
    first labels, run A: [1 1 2 1 1 1 2 1]
first labels, run B: [0 0 2 0 0 0 2 0]
ARI between runs   : 1.0
    A report that says 'segment 2 churns most' breaks on the next refit. Name segments by their profile.
  8. Mistake: more components than rows (1m). "A filtered subset is how this happens." 
    GaussianMixture(n_components=5, random_state=42).fit(X_stretched[:3])
    ValueError: Expected n_samples >= n_components but got n_components = 5, n_samples = 3
  9. Mistake: ignoring a ConvergenceWarning (2m). "With max_iter=2 EM stops half-way and only warns." 
    import warnings
with warnings.catch_warnings(record=True) as caught:
    warnings.simplefilter("always")
    GaussianMixture(n_components=3, max_iter=2, random_state=42).fit(X_stretched)
print(caught[0].category.__name__)
    ConvergenceWarning
    Fix: raise max_iter, scale features, check converged_; try n_init=3 or a larger reg_covar if a component collapses.
Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Stopping at predict. Symptom: a lab answer that only lists component labels. Coach: "How sure is the model about this customer?" Every mixture result today comes with its top probability.
  • Assuming component numbers are stable. Symptom: deal = 1 hard-coded in the discount lab. It happens to be right with seed 42 and wrong with another seed. Coach: "Which component has the larger mean?" (gm.means_.ravel().argmax()).
  • Reading BIC's minimum on real data. Symptom: "BIC is lowest at 8, so 8 segments" (project Step 2, or the lifecycle lab). Coach: "How big is each drop?" Same reading as the elbow in the warm-up.

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud:

  • What do the three learned attributes of a mixture describe? Answer: means_ where each component sits, covariances_ its spread and tilt, weights_ its share of the data.
  • Why can a mixture separate stretched groups that K-Means cuts across? Answer: a full or tied component is an ellipse with its own tilt; K-Means assumes round clusters around centers.
  • A customer gets probabilities 0.51 and 0.49. What do you do with that? Answer: treat them as borderline; keep the probability next to the label; leave them out of narrowly targeted decisions.
  • What tells you a customer is unlike every segment? Answer: a low score_samples (log-density), not a low probability.
