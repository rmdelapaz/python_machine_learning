Lessons

8.6

Anomaly Detection

By the end you can
  • Tell point, contextual and collective anomalies apart and choose between supervised and unsupervised detection
  • Apply simple statistical rules (z-score, interquartile range) as a baseline
  • Score anomalies with Isolation Forest and Local Outlier Factor, and read the -1/1 and score conventions correctly
  • Choose a threshold from a review budget instead of tuning contamination
  • Evaluate a detector with ROC AUC, average precision and catches within a budget on a time-based split
Key ideas
  • The night guard. Learn what normal looks like, score how far each case is from it, review as many top scores as you can afford.
  • Three kinds: point, contextual, collective. Good features (per-category amounts, velocity) turn the last two into the first.
  • Rules (z-score, IQR fence) are transparent baselines that see one column at a time.
  • Isolation Forest: random cuts isolate odd points quickly. LOF: compares a point's density with its neighbors'; needs scaling; novelty=True for new data.
  • Conventions: predict gives -1 (outlier) and 1; score_samples is higher for NORMAL points: negate it.
  • contamination only sets the threshold; choose the threshold from a review budget and costs.
  • Judge with ROC AUC, average precision and catches in the budget, on a time split, against a rule. Unusual is not the same as bad.
Worked example: two rules on a skewed column

200 ordinary order amounts plus three huge ones. Which rule notices what?

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
amounts = np.append(rng.lognormal(4.8, 0.5, size=200).round(2), [2400.0, 2600.0, 3100.0])

z = (amounts - amounts.mean()) / amounts.std(ddof=1)
q1, q3 = np.percentile(amounts, [25, 75])
fence = q3 + 1.5 * (q3 - q1)
print(f"mean {amounts.mean():.1f}, std {amounts.std(ddof=1):.1f} (without the three: {amounts[:200].std(ddof=1):.1f})")
print(f"z-score above 3: {(z > 3).sum()} flagged; smallest flagged {amounts[z > 3].min():.0f}")
print(f"IQR fence ${fence:.0f}: {(amounts > fence).sum()} flagged; smallest flagged {amounts[amounts > fence].min():.0f}")
mean 170.3, std 319.3 (without the three: 65.2)
z-score above 3: 3 flagged; smallest flagged 2400
IQR fence $278: 9 flagged; smallest flagged 288

The three huge orders inflate the standard deviation from 65 to 319, so the z-score rule sees only those three: everything else looks ordinary next to them. The IQR fence uses quartiles, which the extremes cannot drag, so it flags the three plus six genuinely high ordinary orders from the long tail. Neither is "right": the z-score is easily blinded by the outliers it is looking for, and the fence flags a skewed tail. That is why rules are a baseline.

9.1

Principal Component Analysis

By the end you can
  • Explain what a principal component is and why scaling matters
  • Choose the number of components from explained variance
  • Interpret loadings to name what each component captures
  • Test whether PCA before a model helps, inside a pipeline
  • Use reconstruction for compression, denoising and anomaly scores
Key ideas
  • The flashlight. PCA turns the cloud until its shadow is widest; each later component is the widest direction left, at a right angle.
  • Unsupervised, mixed, linear. It never sees the target; each component mixes all columns; directions are straight.
  • Scale first when units differ; do not standardize columns that share one meaningful unit.
  • How many? Scree elbow, a variance target (n_components=0.90), or cross-validation for a model.
  • Loadings (components_) name a component; signs are arbitrary.
  • Most variance is not most useful: on churn, two components lost much of the signal; test PCA inside a pipeline.
  • Reconstruction (inverse_transform) compresses, denoises and gives an anomaly score.
Worked example: three columns that all mean "how much"

Three scaled churn columns that all measure "how much": orders, spend, returns. What does PCA make of them?

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

customers = pd.read_csv("data/customers_churn.csv")
cols = ["orders_12m", "spend_12m", "returns_12m"]
Z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(customers[cols])
pca = PCA().fit(Z)
print("share of variance:", pca.explained_variance_ratio_.round(3))
print(pd.DataFrame(pca.components_.T, index=cols, columns=["PC1", "PC2", "PC3"]).round(2))
share of variance: [0.624 0.239 0.137]
              PC1   PC2   PC3
orders_12m   0.60 -0.48 -0.64
spend_12m    0.63 -0.22  0.75
returns_12m  0.50  0.85 -0.17

PC1 weights all three positively and about equally: "how much this customer buys", 62% of the variance. PC2 sets returns against orders: customers who return a lot for what they buy. PC3 (14%) sets spend against orders: big tickets versus many small orders. Three correlated columns, one dominant shared direction plus two smaller contrasts.

Glossary

TermMeaning
anomaly scoreHow far a case is from normal; higher = more unusual
point / contextual / collective anomalyUnusual alone / only in context / only as a group
Isolation ForestRandom cuts; short average path = anomaly
Local Outlier FactorA point's density compared with its neighbors'; novelty=True scores new data
contaminationExpected share of outliers; sets only the threshold (offset_)
average precisionArea under the precision-recall curve; rewards frauds at the very top
review budgetHow many top scores the team can check; the real threshold
principal componentA direction of maximum remaining variance; a mix of all columns
explained variance ratioA component's share of the total variance
loadingsThe weights in components_; read the large ones, ignore the sign
scree plotVariance share per component; look for an elbow
reconstruction errorThe gap between a row and its rebuild from the kept components
